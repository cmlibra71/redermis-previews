How to read a skincare ingredient list (and find the 1% line)

The ingredient printed biggest on the front of the bottle can legally sit in the part of the list where order means nothing. And you can usually find where that zone starts in about ten seconds.

Quick answer: an ingredient list is ranked from most to least, but Australian rules let a brand stop ranking at the 1% mark. Everything under 1% can appear in any order. So the top of the list tells you what dominates the formula. The tail is a set, not a ranking.

Are skincare ingredients listed in order of concentration?

Yes, down to a point. In Australia, cosmetics have to carry an ingredient list under the Consumer Goods (Cosmetics) Information Standard 2020, made under the Australian Consumer Law. The default rule is simple: ingredients go in descending order by volume or mass. Biggest first.

That makes the first five or so names the most useful part of the label. In most creams, serums and cleansers they make up the bulk of what is in the bottle: the base, the texture, the thing you are mostly putting on your skin.

Water (often written as aqua) in first place is normal. It is not a con. Water is how most creams, lotions and serums hold everything else in a form you can spread. A lightweight serum may be mostly water by design.

What does the 1% rule mean on a skincare label?

Here is the part most people never hear about. The same standard offers brands an alternative layout:

  • ingredients at 1% or more, in descending order;
  • then ingredients under 1%, in any order;
  • then colour additives, in any order, at the end.

Translate that and the tail of the list stops being a ranking. Something listed fifteenth is not necessarily present at a higher level than something listed twentieth. Below the line, position is simply where the brand chose to put it.

That can be where front-of-pack "hero" ingredients live. Not always, and not because anyone is breaking a rule. It is just how the format works.

How to find the 1% line

The label will not mark the line for you, but a few ingredients act as reliable signposts because they are almost always used at low levels.

Phenoxyethanol. A very common preservative. In the European Union it is capped at a maximum of 1% in ready-to-use cosmetics, and formulas sold here are often made to that standard. If you see it, it is very likely under 1%.

Fragrance (parfum). Usually a small amount in a skincare formula, particularly in leave-on face products.

Anything listed after one of these signposts is likely under 1% too. Treat it as a strong clue, not a guarantee. Brands can choose order within the tail, and a brand that ranks its whole list strictly is also allowed to.

Why low is not always useless.

This is not a reason to dismiss everything in the tail. Plenty of ingredients do their job at low levels: preservatives, many antioxidants, and fragrance itself are all designed to work in small amounts.

What position cannot tell you is whether a given ingredient is present at a level that suits its purpose. A stated percentage on the front ("10% niacinamide", for example) tells you far more than a place in the list. And concentration is only one input: the formula base, its pH and how consistently you use the product matter as well.

Names that look scarier than they are.

Ingredient lists use either English names or INCI names (the International Nomenclature of Cosmetic Ingredients), a standardised naming system so the same ingredient reads the same way on labels around the world. Many INCI names are Latin botanical names or chemical names.

  • Butyrospermum Parkii butter is shea butter.
  • Tocopherol is vitamin E.
  • Sodium Hyaluronate is a form of hyaluronic acid.

A long chemical name is not a red flag. It is just a precise one. By the same logic, "chemical-free" means very little: water is a chemical, and so is everything else in the bottle.

What the list cannot tell you.

Fragrance can appear as a single word ("fragrance" or "parfum") even when it is a blend of many components, so the list will not always show you which ones are inside. If you react to scented products, that single word is worth noticing.

Sunscreens are a separate category in Australia: products sold mainly for sun protection are therapeutic goods regulated by the TGA, and most carry an AUST L number. Their labels list the active sun filters with their amounts, which is a different system from the cosmetic ingredient list.

A 30-second label check.

Save this for the next time you are standing in the aisle:

  • Read the first five. That is most of what you are buying.
  • Find the signpost. Locate phenoxyethanol or fragrance (parfum). Treat everything after it as likely under 1%, in no ranked order.
  • Place the hero. Is the ingredient named on the front above or below that signpost?
  • Look for a number. A stated percentage tells you more than any position in the list.
  • Ignore "free-from" and "chemical-free" as signals. They say what is absent, not what is useful.

Ten seconds, maybe thirty. The label stops being a wall of Latin and starts being a map.

Buying fewer, better-understood products is its own form of skin care. That is skin longevity in practice: fewer products, better understood.

Frequently asked questions

Is the first ingredient always the main one? Under the Australian standard, ingredients at 1% or more are listed from most to least, so the first ingredient is the largest by volume or mass. In most creams and serums that is water.

Can an ingredient at exactly 1% go in the unranked tail? No. The any-order section is only for ingredients under 1%. An ingredient at 1% or more still has to be ranked.

Does a low position mean an ingredient does nothing? Not necessarily. Preservatives, many antioxidants and fragrance are designed to work in small amounts. A stated percentage is a better guide than position.

Next time you are in the bathroom, take a look: which product has its hero ingredient below the preservative?
