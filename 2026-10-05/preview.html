What do antioxidants do for skin? Why vitamin C serum goes brown

An antioxidant is not armour. It is a supply, and supplies get used up.

The vitamin C serum on the bathroom shelf. Clear when you bought it. Then amber. Then brown.

That colour change is the antioxidant doing its job, in the bottle instead of on your skin. It is the same chemistry as a cut apple browning on the bench. And it is the most useful clue to a question the label never answers: what do antioxidants do for skin in the first place?

The quick answer: an antioxidant is a molecule that gets spent to stop a chain reaction. Your skin makes and stores its own, daylight uses it up, and a serum is a top-up to that supply. It is not a layer of protection.

What is a free radical?

A free radical is a molecule missing an electron. The proper name for the family is reactive oxygen species. Stripped of the jargon, it means this: an unstable molecule that is one electron short, and takes one from whatever is nearest.

The trouble is what happens next. The neighbour that lost an electron is now unstable too, so it takes one from its own neighbour. The damage passes along like a chain.

An antioxidant is a molecule that can give up an electron and stay stable. It hands one over, and the chain ends there.

Two things follow. First, the antioxidant is changed by the exchange. It has been spent. Second, free radicals are not invaders. They are a normal by-product of cells using oxygen. Not a villain. A matter of balance.

Your skin already keeps its own supply.

Skin does not wait for a serum. It holds vitamin C, vitamin E, glutathione and a set of enzymes whose whole job is to keep that balance.

It also stocks them unevenly. When researchers measured antioxidants in the skin of six healthy volunteers, every one they tested was more concentrated in the epidermis (the thin outer layer) than in the dermis beneath it [1]. Vitamin C and glutathione were several times higher. The authors' reading was simple: the outer layer meets the outside world first, so that is where the supply is kept.

Sunlight spends it.

A second study looked at the very top of the epidermis, the stratum corneum, and what one dose of UV does to it.

The dose was deliberately small: below the level that turns skin pink. In six volunteers, that single exposure reduced the main form of vitamin E in the outermost layer by 45 per cent compared with unexposed skin [2].

The same study found something telling in skin that had not been exposed at all. Vitamin E was lowest at the very surface and highest in the deepest part of that layer [2]. Which is roughly what a budget looks like when the front line is always being drawn on first.

So the supply is spent well before you see anything. No redness, no warning.

Why does vitamin C serum turn brown?

L-ascorbic acid, the pure form of vitamin C, is unstable in water, light and air. It gives up electrons readily. That is what makes it an antioxidant, and also what makes it fragile in a bottle.

Getting it into skin is fussy too. Lab work found it needed an acidic formula (a pH below 3.5) to be absorbed, that uptake peaked at a concentration of 20 per cent, and that skin levels were saturated after three daily applications, then faded with a half-life of about four days [3]. Worth stating plainly: this was pig skin, a standard laboratory model. It measured how much got in. It was not a trial of how anyone's skin looked.

The practical part is easier:

  • Choose opaque or airless packaging over a clear dropper bottle.
  • Store it cool and dark, not on a sunny windowsill.
  • Buy small and use it quickly.
  • If it has turned dark amber or brown, it has largely spent itself.

When should you apply vitamin C, morning or night?

Morning is the logical slot, under sunscreen. Daylight is when the supply gets drawn on, so that is when a top-up has something to do.

That is reasoning from the chemistry, not the result of a head-to-head trial. If mornings do not suit your skin or your routine, night is fine. The order matters more than the hour: serum first, sunscreen over the top, every day.

Do antioxidants protect skin from the sun?

Not in the way sunscreen does.

An antioxidant is a top-up, not a filter. It does not block UV, and it does not replace sunscreen.

The best evidence on this is Australian. In Nambour, Queensland, 903 adults under 55 were randomly assigned to daily or discretionary sunscreen use, and to a beta-carotene supplement or a placebo. After 4.5 years, the daily sunscreen group showed no detectable increase in skin ageing. Beta-carotene showed no overall effect, although the authors noted the trial could not rule out a modest one [4].

Stop the spending first. Top up second.

More is not better.

If antioxidants are good, a bigger dose should be better. It is a reasonable guess, and the evidence does not support it.

A pooled analysis of 68 randomised trials of antioxidant supplements found they did not help people live longer [5]. That is about general health in pill form, not skin, so if you take supplements, your GP or pharmacist is the right person to ask.

Food remains the unglamorous source: fruit, vegetables, nuts, seeds.

Four lines worth keeping.

  • Store it dark and cool. Light, air and warmth spend it before you do.
  • Judge it by colour. Clear to pale straw is fine. Brown is finished.
  • Morning, under sunscreen. That is when the supply gets spent, so it is the logical slot.
  • Treat it as a top-up. Never as protection.

Common questions.

Is a brown vitamin C serum still worth using? Not really. Dark amber or brown means it has largely spent itself, so there is little left to give. Replace it, and store the next one dark, cool and airtight.

Does an antioxidant serum replace sunscreen? No. It does not block UV. Think of sunscreen as stopping the spending and the serum as a top-up.

Does skin make its own antioxidants? Yes. It holds vitamin C, vitamin E, glutathione and a set of enzymes, kept more in the outer layer than in the dermis beneath [1].
