What does moisturiser actually do? The three jobs explained

Most of the work is not adding water. It is keeping it.

Tight again by lunch, even though you moisturised this morning? That is usually not your skin needing more water. It is your skin losing it.

That changes how you read a moisturiser. Most of them do not pour water into your skin. They help it hold on to the water it already has. Three ingredient jobs do that, and once you can spot them, the back of the jar starts to make sense, including why a rich cream can sit heavy on top while a light lotion sinks straight in.

Your skin's water comes from the inside.

The outermost layer of your skin is the stratum corneum: a thin wall of flattened cells, stacked in layers and sealed together with fats. It is thinner than a sheet of paper, and it does most of the work of keeping water in.

That water does not arrive from outside. It rises from the deeper, living layers of skin and moves steadily up and out. The proper name is transepidermal water loss, or TEWL. In plain terms: the slow, constant evaporation of water through your skin's surface, all day and all night.

How much the outer layer keeps depends on the fats between its cells, the winding path water must travel to escape, and a mix of small, water-loving compounds inside the cells called natural moisturising factor [3].

So tight, rough skin usually does not "need a drink". Water is leaving faster than the surface can hold it. Think of a pot simmering with the lid off. Most of what a moisturiser does sits on the holding side.

Humectants pull water in and hold it.

Humectants attract water and hang on to it. The common ones on a label are glycerin, hyaluronic acid (often listed as sodium hyaluronate), urea, panthenol and sodium PCA [1].

Your skin makes its own versions: natural moisturising factor includes PCA and urea [3].

Humectants draw water into the outer layer and help hold it there, so the surface looks and feels more supple [1]. Glycerin is the quiet workhorse: inexpensive, well studied, and in many moisturisers on any shelf.

On their own, humectants have one gap. They gather water but do little to stop it evaporating. That is where the other two jobs come in.

Emollients smooth the surface.

Under magnification, dry skin is not flat. The edges of the outer cells lift and curl, leaving small gaps that scatter light and feel rough under your fingertips.

Emollients fill those gaps. Squalane, plant oils, fatty alcohols such as cetearyl alcohol, and ceramides all do this job [1]. They lay the lifted edges flat, which is why skin can feel softer and smoother within minutes of applying a cream [5].

That softness is a feel-and-texture job, not a hydration job. And a fatty alcohol is not the drying kind: cetearyl and cetyl alcohol are waxy and soft.

Occlusives slow water leaving.

Occlusives are the lid on the pot. They form a thin, water-repelling layer that slows evaporation [1]. Petrolatum (petroleum jelly), dimethicone, beeswax, lanolin and shea butter are the ones you will see most.

Petrolatum is the benchmark. One dermatology review reports that at 5% or more it reduces water loss through the skin by more than 98%, while lanolin, mineral oil and silicones reduce it by roughly 20 to 30% [1].

It also does more than sit on top. In a 1992 study, researchers disrupted the outer skin layer of volunteers under controlled conditions, then applied petroleum jelly. It moved into the spaces between cells throughout the outer layer, and treated sites recovered their barrier faster than untreated ones [4]. A small lab finding, but a telling one.

Which brings us to a common myth: thicker is not "stronger". Thickness mostly tells you the balance has shifted towards emollients and occlusives. A light glycerin lotion and a dense balm are doing different work, not more and less of the same.

How to read the back of your jar.

In Australia, cosmetic ingredients are listed in descending order of concentration, down to about 1%. Below that, they can appear in any order. So the first five or so ingredients tell you the formula's main job.

A quick decoder:

  • Water (aqua) then glycerin near the top: humectant-led. Light and quick to sink in. Often suits humid weather and oilier skin.
  • Plant oils, butters, squalane or fatty alcohols high up: emollient-rich. A softer, creamier feel.
  • Petrolatum, dimethicone, lanolin or beeswax high up: occlusive-led. Richer and more sealing. Often suits cold, dry or air-conditioned air, or skin that tightens soon after washing.
  • All three within the first six: a balanced all-rounder, which is what most everyday moisturisers aim for.

Matching the mix to your skin and the season.

Skin's needs shift with the air around it. As the weather warms and humidity rises, a lighter, humectant-led texture is often enough. When the air turns cold and dry, or heating and air-conditioning run for hours, a richer formula with more occlusive in it often feels better.

One practical habit: apply moisturiser to slightly damp skin, so the humectants have water to hold and the occlusives have something to seal in [1].

A higher price does not buy a better formula.

The job mix matters more than the price. The core ingredients above are inexpensive and well studied. Creams do differ a great deal, and it is the formula, not the name on the front, that shapes how a product behaves on skin [2]. A higher price often buys texture, fragrance or packaging. Those can be worth having, but they sit on top of the three basic jobs, not in place of them.

What moisturiser actually does: the three jobs inside most moisturisers

Moisturised this morning, tight again by lunch? Most moisturisers do not add water to your skin at all.

They help it keep the water it already has.

Your skin's water rises from the inside and evaporates at the surface all day. The proper name is transepidermal water loss. Think of a pot with the lid off.

Dermatology reviews sort moisturiser ingredients into three jobs:

1. Humectants pull water in and hold it: glycerin, hyaluronic acid, urea.
2. Emollients smooth the gaps between surface cells, which is why skin feels softer within minutes: squalane, plant oils, cetearyl alcohol.
3. Occlusives slow the water leaving: petrolatum, dimethicone, shea.

The practical bit: ingredient lists run from most to least, so the first five tell you what your moisturiser mainly does.

And heavy is not stronger. It is just a different mix.

Lighter, humectant-led textures often suit warmer, more humid weather. Richer, occlusive-led ones suit dry, cold or air-conditioned air.
