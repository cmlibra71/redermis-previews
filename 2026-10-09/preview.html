AHA vs BHA: which exfoliating acid does what, and which one raises sun sensitivity

Two exfoliating acids sit side by side on the chemist shelf. In small studies, one of them made skin redden at a lower dose of UV. The bottle rarely says which.

Most people choose an exfoliating acid by the concern printed on the label. The more useful question is what the acid dissolves in. Water or oil. That one property decides where the acid goes, and where it goes decides what it can do.

An acid exfoliant does not scrub. It unglues.

If you know the brick-and-mortar picture of the skin's outer layer, you already have most of this. The bricks are flat, dead cells called corneocytes. They are held to each other by tiny protein rivets called desmosomes, which are the attachment points between one cell and the next.

A scrub knocks bricks off by force. An acid loosens the rivets, so the top cells let go on their own. Nothing is sanded. That is why a well-chosen acid can leave skin looking smoother with far less friction than a grainy scrub.

What is the difference between AHA and BHA?

In short: an AHA dissolves in water and works on the surface, and a BHA dissolves in oil and can travel into the pore. Here is each in turn.

AHAs dissolve in water, so they work on the surface.

Alpha hydroxy acids (AHAs) are water-soluble. They stay in the watery spaces of the upper layers and do their work there. Three turn up most often, and the main difference between them is size.

  • Glycolic acid. The smallest molecule, so it travels furthest and fastest. Often the most noticeable, and the most likely to sting.
  • Lactic acid. Larger and generally gentler. It is also a humectant, meaning it holds water in the skin as well as loosening cells.
  • Mandelic acid. Larger again, and the slowest of the three. Often the easiest starting point for skin that reacts readily.

What AHAs can visibly help with: rough texture, dullness, and uneven-looking tone. Gradually, with consistent use. Not overnight.

BHA dissolves in oil, so it can follow sebum into the pore.

There is one beta hydroxy acid (BHA) in common use: salicylic acid. It is oil-soluble, which changes its route entirely. A pore is lined with sebum, the skin's own oil. A water-soluble acid tends to sit on top of that. An oil-soluble one can mix with it and work along the pore lining, loosening the build-up of cells and oil inside.

That is why salicylic acid tends to suit congested, breakout-prone skin, and why it often does less for surface dullness than an AHA does.

Two cautions. Salicylic acid belongs to the same chemical family as aspirin, so anyone with an aspirin sensitivity should check with a pharmacist or GP first. If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, ask your GP before starting it.

Does glycolic acid make skin more sensitive to the sun?

In small studies, yes. This is the part the bottle rarely makes clear, and it is the most useful difference between the two families.

In a 2003 study, 29 volunteers applied 10 percent glycolic acid (pH 3.5) to an area of their back once daily, six days a week for four weeks, with a placebo on another area. The glycolic-treated skin reddened at a lower dose of UV and formed more sunburn cells (skin cells damaged enough by UV to self-destruct). One week after they stopped, the difference had gone.

A 2009 study put the two families side by side. Fourteen volunteers used 10 percent glycolic acid, 2 percent salicylic acid, or a plain vehicle for three and a half weeks, then were exposed to simulated sunlight. Glycolic acid increased redness, DNA damage and sunburn cells. Salicylic acid produced no significant change in any of the three.

The limits matter. These are small studies, on back skin rather than faces, over a few weeks, at one concentration each. They do not tell us about every AHA at every strength.

The practical translation is still clear. An AHA is an evening product, and it is a good reason to wear sunscreen daily for as long as you use it and for about a week after you stop. Through an Australian summer, that counts for a lot. None of this means salicylic acid users can skip sunscreen. It means one acid adds a known extra reason to wear it.

The number on the bottle is half the story.

Percentage tells you how much acid is in the formula. pH decides how much of it is in its free, active form. The same 10 percent is far more active at a low pH than at a higher one. Think of percentage as the coffee in the tin and pH as how much of it actually gets brewed. Contact time matters just as much: a leave-on product keeps working for hours, while a cleanser is rinsed away in under a minute.

How often should you use AHA or BHA?

Start with one acid, not two. Use it two or three evenings a week, not daily, and give it a few weeks before changing anything.

While your skin is adjusting, keep it off the same night as a retinoid. Both draw on the same irritation budget.

Signs you have overdone it:

  • Tightness that moisturiser does not settle
  • A glassy shine without any oil
  • Stinging from products that never stung before

If that happens, stop the acid, moisturise simply, and restart lower and less often once your skin feels normal. Never apply an acid to broken or sunburnt skin.

If a skin concern is persistent, painful or simply not settling, a pharmacist or GP is the right next step.

Common questions.

Can you use AHA and BHA together? Some products combine them, but it is not the place to start. Each one adds to the same irritation budget. Begin with a single acid, two or three evenings a week, and only think about adding a second once your skin has been comfortable for several weeks. If you do use both, alternate evenings.

Which is better for blackheads, AHA or BHA? BHA is usually the better fit. A blackhead sits inside the pore, and salicylic acid is the one that dissolves in oil, so it can work along the pore lining where the build-up is.

Which is better for dull skin? An AHA. Dullness and rough texture are surface concerns, and AHAs work on the surface layers. Lactic or mandelic acid tends to be the gentler starting point, and glycolic the most noticeable.
