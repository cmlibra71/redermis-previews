Air Pollution and Skin Ageing: What Smoke and Traffic Particles Actually Do

The exposure your skin gets that you never log.

Most people can give you a rough sun history. The teenage summers. The years before SPF became a daily habit. Almost nobody can give you an air history. Yet ageing skin is the running total of everything it has been exposed to, and air is on that list, whether or not anyone has been keeping count.

Every research claim below is named and linked in the reference list at the end, so this is one to save if you like checking the source yourself.

Ageing is a sum of exposures.

Dermatology has a word for this: the exposome. Stripped of the jargon, it means the sum of every external factor your skin meets across a lifetime. Krutmann and colleagues laid out the skin version in 2017: sun, air pollution, temperature, tobacco smoke, diet, sleep and stress. UV is the largest single line item by a wide margin, and that is the only sentence sun gets here. The point of the exposome idea is that the other lines are not zero. Air pollution is one of the better-evidenced ones, and the one almost nobody thinks about.

What is actually in the air.

"Pollution" is vague, so here is the inventory.

PM10 is particles under 10 micrometres across: dust, pollen fragments, road grit. PM2.5 is under 2.5 micrometres, roughly a thirtieth of the width of a human hair, and it is the fraction that comes from combustion: exhaust, wood heaters, bushfires. A subset of PM2.5 is black carbon, which is simply soot. Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, or PAHs, ride on the surface of those soot particles: the tarry compounds produced whenever something organic burns incompletely. Then the gases: ozone at ground level, formed when sunlight cooks traffic emissions, and nitrogen dioxide, largely from vehicles and gas.

The particles are what most people picture. The chemistry riding on them is what matters for skin.

Does air pollution actually get into your skin?

The instinctive worry is penetration: soot working its way down into the skin. That is mostly not what happens. Intact skin is a good gate. The stratum corneum, the outer layer of flattened dead cells sealed together with lipids, stops most particles of that size cold.

The catch is that the particles do not need to cross it. They sit on the surface, and that is enough. Their surface chemistry generates reactive oxygen species right where they land, oxidising the lipids that make up the seal. The PAHs they carry are small enough to move where the particle cannot, and they bind to receptors in living skin cells (the aryl hydrocarbon receptor is the best studied), switching on inflammatory and pigment-related signalling from the outside in. Gu and colleagues reviewed this cascade in 2024: oxidative stress at the surface, receptor signalling below it, barrier disruption and inflammation as the common thread.

So the useful question is not "how deep does it go". It is "how good is the gate". A barrier worn down by over-cleansing, cold dry air or a strong active lets more of the chemistry through. The barrier is the variable that matters more than the particle.

What the population data actually shows.

The SALIA study followed a cohort of older German women and compared their skin against the traffic-related soot and particle exposure at their home addresses. Vierkötter and colleagues reported in 2010 that higher exposure went with more pigment spots on the forehead and cheeks, and with more pronounced nasolabial folds. Observational, so association rather than proof of cause, and pigment is the evidence, not the subject.

Hüls and colleagues extended the analysis in 2016 across both German and Chinese cohorts, and the link between traffic-related pollution and facial lentigines (flat brown spots) appeared in both populations.

The acute end of the same story comes from wildfire smoke. Fadadu and colleagues looked at dermatology visits at clinics well downwind of a major Californian wildfire. During the smoke weeks, visits for itch and for eczema flares rose, in adults and children alike, compared with a clean-air year. That is the barrier being disrupted in real time, at population scale.

The Australian version of this exposure.

Honesty first: Australia's average air quality is good by world standards. The exposure here is episodic rather than constant, and that changes how to think about it. The spikes are what count.

Three of them are predictable. Winter wood-heater smoke: on still, cold nights in valley towns and the outer suburbs of Melbourne, Hobart, Canberra, Armidale and Launceston, cold air drains downhill, pools near the ground and holds the smoke with it, and PM2.5 readings can climb past those of a busy city on a weeknight. Traffic corridors: living or commuting along a main arterial, a tunnel portal or a freight route puts you inside a narrow band of elevated black carbon and nitrogen dioxide that drops off a few streets away. And bushfire smoke, which does not respect distance: a large fire can send a plume that sits over a city a long way downwind for days, as anyone who lived through the summer of 2019 to 2020 remembers.

Every state environment authority publishes a live air quality index, and the hourly PM2.5 reading tells you whether today is a normal day or a dose day. That is your air history, being written.

How to protect your skin from air pollution.

Cleanse once, properly, at the end of an exposure day. The load is on the surface, so a single thorough evening cleanse removes most of it. Cleansing more often wears the barrier and opens the gate.

A morning antioxidant step. The pathway is oxidative, so a topical antioxidant applied before exposure (vitamin C is the best studied) gives the surface something to spend before the lipids are spent.

SPF. UVA and airborne pollutants compound each other, so the sunscreen you already wear is doing double duty on a smoky day.

Protect the barrier. Moisturise, avoid stacking strong actives on a bad-air day, and treat cold dry winter air as part of the same problem. On heavy smoke days, a HEPA purifier, or simply keeping windows shut during the worst hours, is a skin measure as much as a lung one.

The useful reframe.

Pollution is one of the very few ageing inputs that is partly within your control and costs almost nothing to manage. It also accumulates the way a dose does, not the way an event does: no single smoky evening matters, but twenty winters of them do. Skin longevity is mostly about lowering the running total of small insults, and this is one column of the ledger most people have never looked at.

Which is yours: wood smoke, a main road, or bushfire smoke?
