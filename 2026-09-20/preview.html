The pool smell is not chlorine.

Swim season opens this month, and with it the annual complaint: the pool wrecks my skin. The usual answer is chlorine. The real answer is what that chlorine met once it got there: our sweat, our sunscreen, our skin oils.

Water is never just water.

What is dissolved in water decides what the water does to your skin. An Australian summer serves three of them in a single afternoon: chlorinated pool water, sea water, and the hot shower you take to wash the other two off. Each works by a different mechanism. The third one is often the harshest.

The smell is not chlorine. It is what chlorine found.

This is straight pool chemistry, no study required. Free chlorine in water exists mainly as hypochlorous acid, which is close to odourless at pool concentrations. Add nitrogen compounds, and those arrive with us: sweat, skin oils, sunscreen, cosmetics and, unglamorously, urine. Chlorine reacts with the nitrogen to form chloramines: monochloramine, dichloramine and trichloramine.

Trichloramine is the one you can smell. It is volatile and poorly soluble, so it leaves the water and sits in the thin layer of air just above the surface. Which is exactly where your eyes and nose are when you are swimming laps. It is also why an indoor pool with poor air handling smells far stronger than an outdoor one running identical chemistry.

Read that across to the practical point, and it flips the folk belief. A pool that reeks of "chlorine" is usually signalling a higher organic load, not a heavier dose of disinfectant. The smell is a by-product, not the product. Researchers measuring airborne trichloramine across twenty Italian indoor pools found a dose-response relationship between air concentrations and red eyes, itchy eyes, runny nose and voice loss in pool staff (Fantuzzi and colleagues, 2013). The irritant is partly manufactured by the swimmers. Which means some of it is genuinely in our hands.

Why the tightness arrives twenty minutes after, not during.

In the water you feel fine. It is walking to the car that your face feels like it has been ironed.

The reason is that the sensation follows the water loss, not the exposure. Your outermost layer, the stratum corneum, is a brick-and-mortar arrangement: flattened dead cells set in ordered lipid layers. That mortar is what holds water in. Oxidising agents in pool water act on those surface lipids and proteins, and the measurable consequence is that the layer holds water less well for some hours afterwards.

That part has been measured. In a study of forearm immersion in warm water at graded residual chlorine levels, the water-holding capacity of the stratum corneum fell significantly, and it fell at lower chlorine concentrations in people with atopic skin than in people without (Seki and colleagues, 2003). In the non-atopic group, the effect only reached significance at the highest concentration tested. So this is a real effect with a real gradient: dramatic for some skin, mild for most.

The term for the outward leak is transepidermal water loss. Translated: how fast water escapes upward through your skin, measurable with a probe, and higher when the barrier is unsettled. You do not feel the leak. You feel its result, about twenty minutes later.

Two minutes. Before, not after.

Here is the counter-intuitive bit, and it is the part worth remembering.

Rinse in clean water before you get in.

The stratum corneum can only take up so much water. Skin already saturated with clean tap water, ideally with a light moisturiser over it, takes up less pool water, because there is less room left for it to go. Two minutes under a shower before you swim does more for your skin than anything you do in the twenty minutes afterwards.

And it pays twice. The pre-swim shower is what pool operators have been asking for all along, because less sweat, oil and sunscreen going into the water means fewer chloramines forming for everybody in it. The same two minutes protects your barrier and lowers the load in the pool. Rarely does skincare advice also make you a better citizen.

Salt water runs on a different mechanism.

Do not lump the ocean in with the pool. Sea water is hypertonic, saltier than the fluid in your cells, and the film it leaves behind concentrates as it dries in wind and sun. The longer that film sits on you, the more strongly it draws water outward. The salt is not doing much while you are in the water. It does its work on the walk back up the beach.

Interestingly, sea water itself is not a straightforward irritant. In an experimental irritation model, sea water and its sodium and potassium components actually limited the rise in water loss compared with deionised water (Yoshizawa and colleagues, 2001). The drying film is the issue, not the sea. Which gives you a clean rule: rinse reasonably promptly, and do not let the salt dry on you.

The after-swim sequence.

  • Rinse cool to lukewarm. Not hot.
  • Pat dry. Do not rub.
  • Moisturise while skin is still slightly damp, within a few minutes.
  • If your face feels tight that night, skip the retinoid or the acid and resume the next night.

That last one deserves a sentence.

Exposures stack across one summer day: water chemistry, UV, heat, wind, salt. Four of those you cannot control. The strong active on your bathroom shelf is the one input you fully control, so it is the sensible one to move.

What does not help.

A hotter, longer shower to "wash the chlorine off" strips more surface lipid than the pool did. Double cleansing after a swim is usually one cleanse too many, since you have just been immersed in water for an hour. And products sold specifically to remove chlorine are, in practice, doing what a gentle cleanser and a good moisturiser already do.

Two minutes before. Two minutes after. That is most of it.
