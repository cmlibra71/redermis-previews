Does Exercise Help Your Skin? Why You Flush, What May Last

Does exercise help your skin? What a workout does in minutes, and what it may do over months.

Two clocks start when you move. One runs in minutes. The other runs in months.

The quick answer: the red face after exercise is your body releasing heat through widened skin vessels, and it usually settles within an hour. Over months, early research links regular training with better skin elasticity and a firmer supportive layer, though the evidence is still limited.

The red face after a brisk walk up a hill is not damage. It is your radiator. Working muscles make heat, and your body sends warm blood up close to the surface of your skin so that heat can escape into the air.

That flush is the fast clock. There is a second, much slower one: the changes regular movement may make to the structure of your skin over months. Most of the confusion about exercise and skin comes from mixing the two up. People worry the beetroot face is harm, and they hope the glow will last. Neither is quite right.

Why your face goes red when you exercise.

Muscles are not efficient engines. Much of the energy they burn becomes heat, so a brisk walk or a set of squats warms you from the inside.

Your main way to shed that heat is through your skin. Nerves signal the small blood vessels in the skin to widen, a response with a proper name: active cutaneous vasodilation [3]. Stripped of the jargon, your body opens the vents. More warm blood runs close to the surface, and heat passes from skin to air. Sweat adds a second cooling route as it evaporates.

During hard exercise in warm conditions, blood flow to the skin can climb many times above its resting level [3], which is why the change can look so dramatic.

Fair skin and thinner skin let more of that colour show. The cheeks tend to flush first because they are well supplied with small vessels sitting near the surface.

This is normal, and it usually settles within about 30 to 60 minutes of cooling down. A flush that comes with dizziness, a rash or chest symptoms, or one that never settles, is worth checking with your GP.

The post-workout glow is real, and it is temporary.

The glow in the mirror afterwards is the fast clock still winding down. It comes from extra blood flow near the surface and a little moisture on the skin, not from a change in the skin itself. Enjoy it. Just do not expect it to last until dinner.

What regular exercise may do to skin over months.

The slow clock is where it gets interesting, and where the evidence is still early.

A possible signal from muscle to skin.

In 2015, a team at McMaster University in Canada published a study in Aging Cell on exercise and skin ageing in people and mice [1]. They compared habitually active adults with sedentary adults across a wide age range, and found that in active people, particularly the oldest group (65 to 86), the outer layers of the skin looked closer to those of younger adults. When 10 previously sedentary adults in that older group then did three months of twice-weekly cycling, several measures of their skin structure improved.

In mice, the team went looking for a messenger. Working muscle appeared to send out a signalling molecule called IL-15 (interleukin-15), which was linked to how well the energy-producing parts of skin cells, the mitochondria, were working as skin aged [1].

Two cautions: the human groups were small, and most of the mechanism work was in mice.

A trial in middle-aged women.

A 2023 study in Scientific Reports took a more direct approach [2]. Researchers in Japan put 61 healthy, previously sedentary middle-aged women through 16 weeks of either aerobic or resistance training, and analysed data from 56 of them.

In this study, both groups improved in skin elasticity and in the structure of the upper dermis, the supportive layer beneath the surface. The resistance training group also showed an increase in dermal thickness. The authors linked these changes to shifts in circulating factors in the blood, including some involved in inflammation.

It is one trial, in one population, over four months. Some of the authors are employees of a cosmetics company, which the paper declares.

What that adds up to.

Muscle and skin may talk to each other. In these studies, regular movement was associated with measurable changes in skin structure, and resistance work may add something of its own. But this is a long-game input, like sleep or sunscreen. Not a fix, and not something one week of workouts will show.

Does running make your face sag?

You may have heard of "runner's face": the idea that years of bouncing pull the skin down. We could not find any good research behind it.

The more plausible explanations are everyday ones. Keen runners and cyclists clock years of outdoor hours in sun and wind. And some very lean endurance athletes carry little facial fat, which is part of what gives a face its fullness. Neither has much to do with the bounce.

If you train outdoors, early or late in the day, a cap and a sweat-resistant sunscreen cover most of it.

Looking after your skin around a workout.

  • Go in bare-faced or lightly. Heavy layers and sweat do not mix.
  • Rinse with lukewarm water afterwards. No scrubbing: flushed skin does not need more friction.
  • Moisturise once you have cooled down, not while your face is still hot and damp with sweat.
  • Watch the friction points. Chin straps, helmet pads and headbands can rub the same spot every session.

The takeaway: what exercise may do for your skin.

The fast clock is easy to see and gone within the hour. The slow clock is invisible day to day, and it is the one that may count: small signals, repeated over months. The same logic applies to any slow skin habit, whether that is movement or sunscreen.

What does your skin do after a workout: flush, glow, feel tight, or nothing at all? Worth sending to the friend who goes beetroot after a walk.
