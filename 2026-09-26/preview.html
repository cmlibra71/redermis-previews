Does alcohol age your skin? What fades by lunchtime and what adds up over years.

Does alcohol age your skin? What fades by lunchtime and what adds up over years.

That puffy, flushed morning-after face is not mostly dehydration. It is a hormone, your blood vessels and a broken second half of the night, and most of it is gone by lunch.

You know the scene: the morning after a day at the races or a wedding that ran well past the speeches, lids a little puffy and a pink flush still sitting across your cheeks. So what was it, and does any of it stay?

Alcohol shows up in your skin on two clocks. One runs in hours. The other runs in years.

Why is my face puffy after drinking?

You will often hear that it is "just dehydration", but that is only part of the story. Alcohol suppresses vasopressin (also called antidiuretic hormone), the hormone that tells your kidneys to hold on to water. Your fluid balance swings, and overnight some of that fluid can pool where skin is thin and loose, such as under the eyes. So the fluid is not simply leaving. It is moving around, which is why you can feel parched and look puffy at the same time.

Sleep is the other half of it. Alcohol can help you fall asleep faster, but it breaks up the second half of the night, including REM sleep (Ebrahim et al., 2013). A broken night tends to show up in the mirror as exactly that heavier, less rested morning-after look.

A few simple things help the short clock along:

  • Cool water. Splash your face, or rest a cool, damp flannel over your eyes for a minute.
  • Head slightly raised. An extra pillow gives fluid less chance to settle around the eyes overnight.
  • Water with each drink. A glass alongside every drink works better than one big catch-up glass at midnight.
  • Gentle cleansing. Flushed skin does not need scrubbing or a stripping wash to "wake it up". Lukewarm water and a mild cleanser are plenty.

Does alcohol cause skin redness?

In the short term, yes. Alcohol widens small blood vessels near the surface of the skin, a process called vasodilation. More blood moving close to the surface is what gives you that warm, flushed look, and it can linger into the next morning across the cheeks and nose.

For some people the flush is fast and strong. Your body breaks alcohol down into a compound called acetaldehyde, then clears it with an enzyme known as ALDH2. Some people carry a less active version of that enzyme. For them, even one drink can bring it on across the face.

This is genetics, not willpower. If you are the friend who goes pink halfway through the first glass of bubbles, that is simply how your body processes alcohol, and it is useful to know about yourself.

What the long-term studies actually show.

This is where the question "does alcohol age your skin?" gets a more careful answer.

The largest survey on the topic, published by Goodman and colleagues in 2019 (one author is based at Monash University in Melbourne), asked 3,267 women in the US, Australia, Canada and the UK to rate their own faces against photo scales. Heavy drinking, defined as 8 or more drinks a week, was associated with more upper-face lines, under-eye puffiness, downturned mouth corners (oral commissures), midface volume loss and visible blood vessels.

It is worth reading that with clear eyes. The study was cross-sectional, meaning a single snapshot rather than people followed over time. The ratings were self-rated, not measured. The result is an association, not proof that alcohol caused those changes. And the work was funded by Allergan. None of that makes it worthless. It means it points in a direction rather than settling the matter.

The second piece of evidence on alcohol and skin redness comes from a much longer view. Li and colleagues (2017) followed 82,737 women in the Nurses' Health Study II for 14 years. Higher alcohol intake was associated with a higher risk of rosacea, and white wine and spirits showed the clearest trend. Again, that is "associated with", not "causes": observational research can spot a pattern, but it cannot rule out everything else going on in people's lives.

So here is the plain ceiling. The links in these studies are with regular, heavier drinking over years. There is no evidence that one big night ages your face. The long clock is about habits, not a single wedding.

What helps skin through a big season.

Spring in Australia can mean a run of long lunches, marquees and late finishes. A few habits worth saving for the weeks ahead:

  1. Pace and alternate. A glass of water between drinks keeps the fluid swing smaller and the evening longer.
  2. Notice your own flush pattern. If your face colours quickly, you already know your skin's early signal. Use it as information, not a verdict.
  3. SPF still matters at an outdoor race day. Spring sun is stronger than it feels on a breezy lawn. A broad-brimmed hat and reapplied sunscreen protect skin whatever is in your glass.
  4. Protect sleep on the night after. An early, uneventful night gives the short clock room to run its course.
  5. Keep the evening routine calm and consistent. Flushed, tired skin rarely thanks you for piling on acids and retinoids to "fix" it. Cleanse gently, moisturise, and let the basics do their work.
