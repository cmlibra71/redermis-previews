Crepey eyelids and crow's feet: why the skin around your eyes ages first

Meta description: What makes eye-area skin different, why spring hay fever matters, and the gentle habits that genuinely help the thinnest skin on your face.

The thinnest skin on your face does the most work

Near your lash line, your skin is about a third of a millimetre thick, and you fold it thousands of times a day.

The thinness alone would make it worth handling gently. But there is a turn: this is also the part of your face you move most. Every blink, squint and smile folds it. And in an Australian spring, a lot of us add a third thing to the list: rubbing.

So crepey eyelids and fine lines at the outer corner are not simply a sign of skin being "delicate". They are, more than anything, a mechanics story. Once you can see the mechanics, it gets much easier to tell which habits are worth keeping.

It is thin, and it is thinner where it folds.

In 2006, a small anatomical study measured upper eyelid skin at different heights. Near the lash margin it averaged about 320 micrometres. Just below the brow, it was closer to 1.1 millimetres, more than three times thicker (Hwang, Kim and Hwang, 2006).

To picture it (an analogy, not a measurement): the skin right at your lash line is roughly as thick as three sheets of printer paper stacked together.

One caveat worth keeping. That study used 10 cadavers from a Korean sample, so treat the figure as a useful reference point rather than a universal number. Skin thickness varies between people, ages and populations. The pattern, though, is the part that matters: the skin is thinnest exactly where the lid folds most.

It moves more than any other skin you own.

A 1997 study recorded blink rates in 150 healthy volunteers. At rest, they averaged about 17 blinks a minute. In conversation, that rose to 26. While reading, it dropped to around 4.5 (Bentivoglio et al., 1997).

Now some back-of-envelope arithmetic (ours, not the paper's): 17 blinks a minute, times 60 minutes, times 16 waking hours, comes to more than 16,000 blinks a day. Add conversation, squinting into bright light and every genuine smile, and the eye area is folding along the same few lines all day, every day.

Here is why that matters. Young skin springs back after each fold. Over the years, skin tends to lose some of that elastic recoil. Repeated folding plus slower recoil helps explain why fine lines tend to settle at the outer corner first, in the zone we call crow's feet. It is not that this skin is failing. It is doing more work, with less material, than almost anywhere else on your face.

Three different things change around the eye.

A 2025 narrative review of periocular ageing (the medical term for the area around the eye) makes the point that change here is multifactorial. Skin, fat, muscle and the connective tissue layer called fascia can all descend over time, and the underlying bone can gradually lose mass. The review also notes real variation between individuals and between populations (Tao et al., 2025).

Translated into plain terms, three layers are involved:

  • Surface: the skin itself. Crepiness, fine lines, texture and tone.
  • Volume: the soft tissue beneath. Hollows, puffiness, the shape of the under-eye.
  • Structure: the bone and ligament support that holds everything in place.

This is where a gentle myth-bust helps. A topical product works at the surface layer. That is useful, and a good eye product can help the skin look smoother and better hydrated. But no cream refills a hollow or lifts a brow, because those changes live in layers a cream does not reach. Knowing which layer you are looking at saves a lot of money and a lot of disappointment.

Why does hay fever season make eye creases worse?

Hay fever season is here across much of Australia, and itchy, watery eyes come with it. The natural response is to rub. A 2019 review of ocular allergy describes how itch and watery-eye sensations drive a chronic rubbing habit, and how vigorous rubbing carries consequences for the eye itself (Ben-Eli, Erdinest and Solomon, 2019). In short, rubbing is hard on more than skin.

Bring that back to the eyelid. Thin skin, plus friction, plus inflammation from the allergy itself, is a poor mix. Every knuckle-rub drags the thinnest skin on your face sideways, often many times a day for weeks.

If the itch is persistent, it is worth talking to your GP, pharmacist or optometrist. Getting the itch under control is often the single most useful thing you can do for the skin around your eyes this season.

Gentle habits that help.

None of these are dramatic. That is the point.

  1. Pat, do not drag. Apply anything around the eye with your ring finger, which naturally uses the least pressure. Tap it in rather than smoothing it across.
  2. Dissolve, do not scrub. Soak a cotton pad in remover and hold it over closed eyes for a few seconds so make-up lifts away, then wipe gently downward once.
  3. Cold, not knuckles. When the itch hits, a cool compress or a clean, chilled damp cloth is kinder than rubbing.
  4. Shade your eyes outdoors. Sunglasses and a broad-brimmed hat cut down on both sun and squinting. It is simple, general advice, and it adds up over a lifetime.
  5. Keep it fragrance-light. The eye area tends to react more readily, so choose gentle, low-fragrance products and patch-test anything new before using it there.

Less friction. More patience.

The eye area rewards consistency over intensity.

It is tempting to treat the skin around the eyes as a problem to be fixed quickly. The anatomy suggests a different approach. This is thin skin under constant motion, and in spring it is under extra friction too. What tends to serve it best over years is not a harsh intervention but fewer daily insults: less rubbing, less squinting, less dragging, a little more protection.

Changes here happen slowly, so improvements from better habits happen slowly too. Think of it as skin longevity rather than a race. Small, gentle, repeated choices are the ones that compound.
