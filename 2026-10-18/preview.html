Why Is My Hair Shedding? The Three-Month Lag, and Why Your Scalp Is Skin Too

The hair in your shower drain today may be about something that happened three months ago. That is not a quirk. It is how the hair cycle is built, and it makes more sense once you stop thinking of your scalp as "hair" and start thinking of it as skin.

Your face does not stop at the hairline.

Your scalp is not a separate organ. It is the same continuous sheet of skin as your forehead, with the same layers: an outer epidermis, a dermis beneath it, and fat under that. The difference is density. Scalp skin carries far more hair follicles and oil glands for its size, and it sits mostly out of sight.

That is why dermatologists increasingly talk about caring for hair and scalp the way you care for facial skin. One 2020 review in Skin Appendage Disorders argues for exactly that idea: a "total facial skin and hair care" approach, where the scalp is part of the same system rather than an afterthought (Calvo Peretti et al., 2020). If you have read about how photoageing differs from chronological ageing, the same logic applies upstairs: sun and time act on the skin wherever it is, hair or no hair.

Three everyday scalp worries make sense once you hold that picture.

Why is my hair shedding? (telogen effluvium)

Each hair follows a cycle. Plain-language version:

  • Growing phase (anagen). Lasts years. Most of your hairs are here at any moment.
  • Transition (catagen). A short wind-down.
  • Resting phase (telogen). Roughly three months, after which the hair is shed and a new one starts.

Now add a shock to the system: a fever or illness, childbirth, a crash diet, a hard stretch of stress. Any of these can push many follicles into the resting phase at once. Because rest lasts around three months, the extra shedding shows up about two to three months after the trigger. This pattern is called telogen effluvium, and it was set out in detail in a classic review (Headington, 1993).

The practical value is in the timing. When you notice more hair on the brush, look back a season, not a week. The cause is often something that has already passed. Shedding of this kind typically settles once the trigger is gone and the cycle resets, though it can take a while to feel like it.

Is dandruff just a dry scalp?

It is natural to assume flakes mean dryness, so people scrub harder or wash less. Neither tends to help.

Dandruff is better understood as a three-way interaction. A yeast called Malassezia lives on virtually everyone's scalp. It feeds on the skin's natural oils (sebum). And some people's skin reacts to the by-products of that feeding with irritation and flaking. Researchers describe these as three facets: the fungi, the oils, and individual sensitivity (DeAngelis et al., 2005).

That explains why moisturising the scalp tends not to solve it, and why harsh scrubbing can leave skin more irritated rather than less. A pharmacy anti-dandruff shampoo with an antifungal ingredient, left on the scalp for the few minutes the label suggests, is the usual first step; ask your pharmacist or GP which one suits you.

Do you need sunscreen on your scalp?

Face skin gets sunscreen in the morning. The scalp, especially along a parting, at the crown or where hair is thinner, often gets direct Australian sun with no protection at all. Because you cannot see it, you rarely notice until it is sore.

Simple ways to cover it:

  • A broad-brimmed hat is the easiest option and covers the area you cannot reach with a mirror.
  • Scalp-friendly sunscreen comes as sprays, gels and powders made to go on without leaving hair greasy.
  • Vary your parting now and then so the same strip of skin is not always exposed.
  • Ask someone to look at your scalp occasionally, or have a skin check include it.

It is worth the small effort. In a large US cancer-registry analysis, people with melanoma of the scalp or neck had poorer survival than people with melanoma at other sites (Lachiewicz et al., 2008). That is not a reason to worry, just a reason to include the scalp in your routine and in your skin checks.

A simple scalp care routine.

Keep it minimal. Three plain steps:

  1. Wash as often as your scalp's oil asks for, not by a fixed rule. Some people need daily, some every few days.
  2. If you need an anti-dandruff shampoo, leave it on long enough to work.
  3. Wear a hat or use scalp SPF when the UV is high.

When to see your GP.

  • Shedding that is sudden or patchy, or that comes with scalp pain or redness.
  • Shedding that carries on for more than about six months.
  • Flaky patches that stay red, greasy or itchy despite a pharmacy shampoo.
  • Any new or changing spot on your scalp, as anywhere else on your skin.

A GP can look for causes that a guess from the bathroom mirror cannot.

The scalp ages with the rest of you. Giving it the same basic care you give your face is a quiet investment in skin longevity, and it costs almost nothing.
