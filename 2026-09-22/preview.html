Why scars fade slowly: what a scar is actually made of

Why don't stretch marks go away? The answer sits in how skin chooses to heal, and it explains why an entire category of creams appears to work.

Walk down the chemist aisle and the scar and stretch-mark creams all make a similar offer: use this daily for 12 weeks, or 12 months, and watch the mark fade. Every one of them shares the same quiet advantage. Scars fade on their own over almost exactly that window. Hold onto that thought, because to see why it matters you need to know what is actually happening in the tissue underneath.

Skin has two answers to damage, and it retired one of them.

When skin is injured, there are two possible responses. The first is regeneration: rebuild the tissue exactly as it was, indistinguishable from the original. The second is repair: close the gap fast with a patch, and worry about fidelity later. Or never.

Adult human skin almost always chooses the patch.

That is not a failure. It is a speed decision. An open wound is a survival problem (infection, fluid loss), while a slightly imperfect closed wound is not. So the body optimises for shutting the door quickly, and accepts a scar as the price.

We did not always work this way. Early-gestation foetal skin can still regenerate, healing without a scar at all. The difference is not a missing ingredient. It sits in the composition of the matrix the new tissue is built into, and in a much quieter inflammatory response. Somewhere before birth, the system trades fidelity for speed, and the trade is permanent (Gurtner et al. 2008; Gomes et al. 2025).

A scar is the same material in a different arrangement.

This is the spine of the whole subject, so it is worth stating plainly.

A scar is not made of different stuff. It is made of the same collagen fibres as the dermis around it. What changes is the arrangement.

Healthy dermis holds its fibres in a loose, randomly oriented mesh. They run in every direction, with open space between them. Scar tissue holds the same fibres packed densely and strictly parallel, all pointing the same way. That difference is not a matter of impression. It has been measured objectively in histology comparing normal skin with normotrophic scar, hypertrophic scar and keloid, and the degree of alignment tracks with how abnormal the scar looks and behaves (Verhaegen et al. 2009).

An everyday picture: a randomly tangled pile of fine wire gives a little in any direction you push it. A combed bundle of the same wire gives only along one axis. Same wire, completely different behaviour.

Everything you notice about a scar falls out of that arrangement.

Once you know the fibres are aligned rather than random, the familiar features of a scar stop being mysterious.

Stiffness. Aligned fibres have fewer directions to give in, so a scar pulls rather than stretches. The everyday proof is an old scar across a knuckle or knee that tugs when you bend.

Shine. Parallel fibres under a flattened surface return light more uniformly than the fine, irregular texture of normal skin, so a scar reads glassy or slightly shiny next to the skin around it. You can see it in most old scars under a bathroom light.

Missing structures. Repair rebuilds the sheet, not the fittings inside it. Inside a mature scar there are no hair follicles, no sweat glands and no oil glands. That is your self-check, and the line worth remembering: an old scar does not grow hair, does not sweat and stays dry.

Stretch marks are scars without wounds.

Stretch marks (striae) confuse people because nothing ever broke the surface. There was no cut, no graze, no scab. Yet they behave like scars: pale, slightly sunken, faintly shiny, permanent.

The explanation is that the tear happened one layer down. The dermis gave way under rapid stretching while the epidermis above it stayed intact, so the repair response ran exactly as it would for a wound, just with the lid still on (Borrelli et al. 2021). The title of that paper carries the whole idea: scars without wounds.

The practical conclusion follows directly. The damage is structural and it sits beneath an unbroken surface. A product applied to the top of intact skin is not reaching that structure in any meaningful sense.

The red-to-white clock.

Here is the part the whole category rests on.

A new scar, and a new stretch mark, looks red or purple. That colour is vascular: the repair tissue is thin and full of fresh blood vessels, and you are seeing through to them. Over months, and up to a couple of years, those vessels regress. The tissue settles pale, silvery and flatter. That is the natural course of scar maturation, and it runs on its own schedule regardless of what you do.

Now put that next to a 12-week or 12-month course of anything applied faithfully across the same window. It will always appear to work. The fade was already coming.

No brand needs to be accused of anything for this to be true. It is simply what happens when a product's timeline and a tissue's timeline overlap. Read the timeline, then do the arithmetic yourself.

What genuinely changes how a scar looks.

Short and hedged, because this is where over-promising usually starts.

  • Time. Do not judge a new scar in its first year. The colour you dislike most is the part most likely to leave by itself.
  • Sun protection. Fresh repair tissue marks more easily than settled skin, so sunscreen over a new scar or stretch mark is one of the few genuinely useful habits.
  • Suppleness and occlusion. Keeping a scar hydrated and covered tends to help it feel more comfortable and look flatter as it matures. Silicone gel sheeting is the one option on the shelf with a body of trial evidence behind it, and a Cochrane review of that evidence found a possible reduction in scar thickness and colour but rated the trials as poor quality and highly susceptible to bias, so treat it as worth trying rather than settled (O'Brien and Jones 2013).

And the honest ceiling, stated once: nothing on a shelf re-randomises the fibre arrangement. The realistic goal is appearance (colour, flatness, flexibility, comfort), not removal. You are managing the finish, not rebuilding the fabric underneath.

Sort your concerns before you spend on them.

Skin's default is speed over perfection. It has been that way since before you were born, and it will not change for a cream.

The most useful thing you can do with that knowledge is sort your own skin concerns into two piles. Structural concerns are permanent architecture: scars, striae, the arrangement of fibres. Functional concerns are things that genuinely move: hydration, circulation, tone, turnover, the way light bounces off the surface. The first pile responds to time and to managing the finish. The second pile responds to what you do daily. Knowing which pile you are looking at, before you spend a dollar, is the single most useful sorting you can do.
