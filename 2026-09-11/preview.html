Why Oily Skin Can Still Be Dehydrated (And What To Do)

By lunchtime your forehead has gone shiny. By nine at night, twenty minutes after cleansing, your cheeks feel tight enough that you keep touching them. Somewhere along the way you were told those two things cannot both be true, or that "dehydrated oily skin" is a phrase invented to sell you a second serum.

It is neither impossible nor invented. It is measurable. Your skin makes oil. It does not make water.

> A lid is not a tap.

Which is the whole thing in one line. Your skin is not producing "oil" in the way a bottle produces oil. It is producing a specific recipe, and the recipe changes.

The confusion comes from the instrument we use to judge oily skin. Almost every routine built for it is chasing a number, which is how much shine there is at hour one. The thing that actually governs comfort, surface condition and how breakout-prone skin behaves is a composition: what that oil is made of, and how long it sits there.

Why is my skin oily and dehydrated at the same time?

Start with the term. Sebum is the output of the sebaceous gland, and it is not the same substance as the lipids your skin cells make on their own.

Human sebum is a distinctive mixture. It contains lipids found essentially nowhere else on the body, notably squalene and wax esters, which do not appear among the epidermal surface lipids the keratinocytes themselves produce (Picardo 2009). Two different sources, two different recipes, mixed together on the surface of your face.

That distinction sounds academic. It changes how you read your own skin. The sebaceous gland is not a moisturiser dispenser sitting there topping you up. It is a gland with an output, and that output has a formula. A formula can change without the volume changing at all.

Oily skin vs dehydrated skin: what is the difference?

This is the load-bearing fact of the whole piece.

In a comparative study of Thai women, researchers measured facial sebum and stratum corneum hydration in women who described their skin as oily and in women who described it as dry. The oily group measured 1.6 to 2.1 times more sebum. Their hydration measured significantly lower. Hydration also correlated negatively with casual sebum level, meaning the more oil sitting on the surface, the less water measured in the layer beneath it (Thadanipon and Kitsongsermthon 2020). The same paper looked at pore size, which is a separate conversation.

Translated plainly: lipid slows water leaving. It does not supply water. Oily is a reading from one system, dehydrated is a reading from another, and one face can return both.

Two honest caveats. That is one study in one population, and a correlation is not a cause. But it is enough to retire the idea that shine and dehydration are mutually exclusive, because someone put probes on faces and found them sitting together.

The recipe changes, not just the volume.

Here is where composition earns its keep.

In work analysing skin surface lipids in women, the older group produced less sebum overall, yet the relative make-up of what they did produce had shifted, with 2,3-oxidosqualene measurably increased (Rigal 2020). Less oil, different oil.

And oxidised squalene does not sit there inertly. In cultured keratinocytes, squalene peroxidation products activated NF-kB (a control switch for inflammatory signalling) and raised IL-6, one of the messenger molecules that switch runs (Ottaviani 2006).

Hedge that properly, twice. Those are cells in a dish, not a face. And a mechanism that is plausible in a lab is not proof of what is happening in your skin on a Tuesday.

Still, it suggests a more useful variable than the one most of us track. Not how much oil, but how long that oil sits on the surface being worked on by UV and airborne oxidants before it comes off.

Why does skin get more dehydrated with age?

The last piece explains the face that makes no sense at forty-five.

Across a life the number of sebaceous glands stays much the same. Sebum output tends to fall after menopause in women. And slower sebocyte turnover in later life leads to enlargement of facial sebaceous glands rather than their disappearance (Zouboulis and Boschnakow 2001).

So the same glands are still there, still visible, arguably more visible, while producing less. Shiny down the T-zone. Tight everywhere else. Individual glands easier to see than they were at twenty-five.

It also explains why the teenage instinct misfires. Stripping made a kind of sense when output was high. The output has already fallen. Stripping it now removes lipid you are no longer replacing quickly.

How to treat oily dehydrated skin

Nothing here is expensive or new. It is mostly about swapping the instrument.

  • One proper cleanse at the end of the day. The point is removing the day's oxidised surface film, not cleansing until the skin squeaks. Squeaking is a sign you overshot.
  • Go easier on stripping surfactants and strong astringents. They are still aimed at volume, and water loss is not a volume problem. Removing lipid to fix dryness runs the trade the wrong way.
  • Add water, then hold it. Humectants onto damp skin, then an emollient over the top. Sebum slows water leaving but supplies none of it, so the water has to come from your routine.
  • A morning antioxidant and sunscreen. These do more for the condition of the oil on your face than any mattifier, because they act on the oxidation side of the problem rather than the volume side.
  • Change the test. Judge your skin on comfort at hour six, not shine at hour one. Shine at hour one is a measurement of almost nothing.

Oil is infrastructure.

Sebum carries antioxidants to the surface, keeps the outer layer pliable and slows water leaving a structure that has no other way of holding it. It is not the enemy. It is a system with an output, a composition and a shelf life, and the useful questions are about quality and dwell time rather than quantity.

Treat it as infrastructure and most of the contradictions dissolve. Shiny and tight stop being a paradox and start being two separate readings from two separate systems, one lipid and one water, that were never the same measurement.

So here is the question worth sitting with. If you stopped measuring your skin by how it looks at lunchtime and started measuring it by how it feels at dinner, what would you actually change first?
