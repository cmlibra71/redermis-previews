Sweat is a cooling system, not a cleaning system.

Does sweat detox your skin? No. Here is what it is actually doing.

You finish a session, catch yourself in the mirror, and there it is: flushed, slick, a face that looks emptied out. Nothing has been emptied. Sweat is a coolant, and cooling is the entire job.

Water. Salt. Heat leaving. That is the whole design brief, and nearly every popular belief about sweat quietly hands it a second job it was never built to do.

What sweat is actually made of

The sweat that matters for temperature comes from eccrine glands, the small coiled glands spread across most of the body's surface. Baker and Wolfe (2020) set out the composition plainly: eccrine sweat is overwhelmingly water, with sodium and chloride as the main electrolytes, plus far smaller amounts of potassium, lactate, urea and ammonia.

Read that list again, because it is where the detox story comes apart. Those are ordinary metabolic small molecules, present in tiny quantities, carried along by the water rather than being the reason for it. Clearing waste is the work of the liver and the kidneys, continuously, whether you train or not. "Sweating out toxins" describes a job sweat is not built to do.

That is not a dig at anyone who has believed it. A hard session genuinely feels like an emptying, and the feeling is real. It is just not a description of what the fluid is carrying.

Evaporation is the cooling step, not the sweating

Here is the part almost everyone skips. Sweat does not cool you. Evaporation cools you.

Heat leaves the body when liquid water on the surface turns into vapour, because that change costs energy, and the energy is taken out of your skin. A bead that rolls off your jaw or soaks into your shirt did essentially no cooling. It was produced, it went somewhere else, and your thermostat got nothing back for it.

The full system runs in two stages: blood flow carries heat from the core out to the skin, and evaporation at the surface carries it away from there (Kenney and Johnson, 1992). Both halves have to work. If the second half stalls, the first one just keeps delivering heat to a surface that cannot shed it.

That single fact explains something plenty of Australians already know in their bodies. A humid Brisbane or Sydney morning is punishing in a way a dry day at the same temperature is not. The air is already loaded with water, so the vapour has nowhere to go, evaporation slows, and the body answers the only way it knows, by producing more sweat that still cannot do its job. You end up wetter and no cooler. Airflow and dry air do far more for how you feel than sweating harder ever will. A fan on a humid morning is not a comfort item, it is a functional one.

Sweating switches on in an order

The second thing sweat is not is uniform. Kondo and colleagues (1998) looked at what changes as exercise intensity climbs, and found two things happening at once: more sweat glands become activated, and the output from each active gland rises. The response also differed by body region.

That recruitment order is why the forehead and trunk feel like they go first while the forearms lag well behind. Your face is not the main event. It is an early recruit. The body brings surfaces online in a sequence as demand rises, and the face sits near the front of the queue.

So the drenched-forehead feeling is not a message about your face specifically, or your pores, or how much you needed that session. It is a message about sequence.

Worth naming while we are here: pores do not open and close. There is no muscle around them. Heat can make surface oil thinner and more mobile, which changes how a pore looks and feels, but nothing has swung open and nothing is being flushed out of it.

The instinct that sweat is dirty

This is the one that costs people the most. Schittek and colleagues (2001) identified dermcidin, an antimicrobial peptide produced by the sweat glands themselves and secreted onto the skin surface with sweat. It is made constitutively, meaning the glands produce it as standard rather than only in response to an injury or an infection.

Sweat, in other words, arrives with antimicrobial activity already in it. Skin is not soiled by the act of sweating. That matters, because "I am filthy" is the justification for the aggressive post-workout scrub, and the scrub is the actual mistake in most post-exercise routines.

What is genuinely worth doing

Rinse rather than scrub. Lukewarm water and a gentle cleanser lift salt and surface oil without you going after a barrier that has just spent forty minutes working hard.

Then there is the hour afterwards. Do not leave damp, salted skin sitting under a cap, a headband, a strap, or a phone held against your cheek. Warmth plus staying damp under a covering is the combination athletes have long been described as reacting to (Basler, 1992). Take the covering off, get dry, let air move across your skin. A plain rinse and a proper dry beats any product decision available to you here.

The unglamorous part

Oizumi and colleagues (2024) published a narrative review on exercise and skin function, which is worth describing accurately: a narrative review gathers and summarises existing literature. It is not a trial, and it does not establish a specific result for a specific person. What it points at is reasonable enough, that exercise belongs in the conversation about how skin looks and functions over years.

Which is the least glamorous claim on this page and probably the most reliable one. Anything you add on top, light-based devices included, sits in the same category: a small repeated input. And it is worth saying plainly, a cosmetic device, not medicine.

The goal was never to sweat more. It is to keep going.

Which part of you starts sweating first, and what is the one thing you always do to your face afterwards? I want to know how many of us are still scrubbing.

Two questions people actually ask.

Does sweating detox your skin? No. Eccrine sweat is overwhelmingly water and salt, with only trace amounts of ordinary metabolic small molecules carried along for the ride. Clearing waste is the liver's and the kidneys' continuous work, not the skin's, and it happens whether you train or not.

Do pores open when you sweat? No, because there is no muscle around a pore to open or close it. Heat makes surface oil thinner and more mobile, so a pore can look and feel different after a session, but nothing has swung open and nothing has been flushed out. Sweat leaves through its own duct, not by forcing a pore.
