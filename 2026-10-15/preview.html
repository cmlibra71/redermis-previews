What makes a face look its age? It is not only the wrinkles.

In one study, people were shown cropped patches of women's cheek skin and asked to guess their age. The more even the colour, the younger the guess.

No eyes. No smile. No hairline. Just a small square of cheek, and strangers still read age from it.

The short version.

  • People guess age from more than lines: colour evenness and facial contrast carry real weight too.
  • More even skin colour (less brown patchiness and redness) was rated younger in blind tests.
  • Daily broad-spectrum sunscreen is one of the biggest levers on evenness that you control.

We judge age quickly, and we use more than lines to do it. Researchers call the result perceived age: the age other people guess you are when they look at you, as opposed to the number on your birth certificate. It is a research field in its own right, and the method is simple. Show photos to a panel of strangers, collect their guesses, then work out which features of the skin pushed those guesses up or down.

Lines matter. But two quieter cues carry real weight: how even the skin's colour is, and how much contrast there is between the features (lips, eyes, brows) and the skin around them.

Does uneven skin tone make you look older?

Matts and colleagues (2007) took cropped cheek images from 170 girls and women aged 11 to 76 and had them rated, blind, by 353 people. The raters never saw a whole face. Even so, skin with more even colour was rated younger, healthier and more attractive, and earned a lower estimated age.

The unevenness came from two pigments:

  • Melanin is the brown pigment skin makes in response to sun. Spread unevenly, it shows up as brown spots and patchiness.
  • Haemoglobin is the red pigment in your blood. Seen through the skin, it shows up as redness and small visible vessels.

Neither pigment is a problem in itself. It is the patchwork, brown clustered here and red clustered there, that the eye seems to read as years.

Why does facial contrast change how old you look?

Porcheron, Mauger and Russell (2013) looked at a different cue: the contrast between facial features and the surrounding skin. Think of how the lips stand out against the skin around the mouth, or how the eyes and brows stand out against the forehead and cheeks.

They found this contrast decreased with age, and that it tracked how old faces were judged. Then they tested it directly. When they digitally raised the contrast, the same faces looked younger. When they lowered it, the same faces looked older.

The authors suggest this is part of why makeup works the way it does. Lipstick, brow pencil and eyeliner all deepen the features against the skin. People have been doing it for centuries; now there is a tidy explanation for why it works.

Do wrinkles still matter?

None of this makes wrinkles irrelevant. Samson, Fink and Matts (2011) worked with the face of a single 61-year-old woman and digitally altered her skin. Smoothing the surface texture (the lines) moved perceived age more. Smoothing the colour unevenness moved perceived health more. Both cues mattered; they simply pulled on different judgements.

A larger study by Nkengne and colleagues (2008) photographed 173 women aged 20 to 74 and had their ages estimated by 48 graders. The eye area and the uniformity of skin colour were the main attributes linked to perceived age.

So the honest picture is that several cues stack. Lines, evenness, contrast, the eye area. No single fix moves all of them, and anything promising otherwise is overselling.

Why do some women look younger than their age?

Why can two women of the same age read years apart? Gunn and colleagues (2009) studied Danish twins aged 59 to 81 alongside British women aged 45 to 75. Wrinkling, hair greying and lip height were each linked to looking older or younger for their age, and the appearance of sun damage was linked too. Comparing twins let them estimate that pigmented age spots, wrinkles and the appearance of sun damage were shaped roughly equally by genes and environment, while greying was mostly genes.

The environmental half is the useful part. A few general, practical takeaways:

  • Daily broad-spectrum sunscreen is one of the biggest levers on brown spots and evenness. Australia has some of the highest UV levels in the world, and sun exposure is a major driver of uneven pigment. Apply it every morning, all year round, not only on beach days.
  • Be gentle with redness-prone skin. Very hot water, harsh scrubs and stacking strong actives can leave skin looking redder. A calm, simple routine helps skin look calmer.
  • Hats, shade and sunglasses count too, especially around the eyes, the area the research keeps pointing back to.

And the reassuring part: a calm, even complexion reads well at any age. This is skin longevity, looking well for your years, not a war on ageing.

A few caveats.

These studies mostly used photographs of Caucasian women, so the findings may not transfer neatly to every skin tone. Several were run by or with cosmetics-industry research centres (authors had P&G, CHANEL, Unilever and J&J affiliations). And they show correlation: they do not prove that changing one cue changes how you are seen in everyday life.

Across these studies, people read age from more than lines. Evenness is the cue daily sun protection looks after best.
