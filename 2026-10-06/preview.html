Keratosis pilaris (chicken skin): why your arms are bumpy and what actually helps

Those bumps on the backs of your arms are not dirt. They are keratosis pilaris, a hair-follicle quirk.

Run a hand down the back of your upper arm. If the skin there feels like fine sandpaper, a scatter of small, firm bumps that never quite come to a head, you already know the texture this article is about. It can turn up on the thighs too, and sometimes on the cheeks. Look closely and there is a pattern: each bump sits exactly where a hair leaves the skin.

Keratosis pilaris is often shortened to KP. Most people call it chicken skin. Stripped of the jargon, it is this: a plug of keratin, the skin's own structural protein, sitting in the mouth of a hair follicle.

The short version:

  • Do: moisturise every day on damp skin, and add a leave-on softening lotion (urea, lactic acid or salicylic acid).
  • Don't: scrub, dry brush or pick. Friction tends to make the same bumps redder.
  • Expect: softer, smoother-feeling skin over several weeks of steady use. It is maintenance, and the bumps return if you stop.

Is keratosis pilaris dirt, acne or contagious?

No, on all three counts. It is not a hygiene problem and not an allergy either. You did not catch it, and nobody can catch it from you.

KP is a very common, harmless trait that often runs in families. It says nothing about how well you wash. Plenty of people carry it for decades without ever hearing its name, which is part of why it gets mistaken for something that needs scrubbing away.

Who gets keratosis pilaris, and where does it show up?

The most useful picture of everyday KP comes from a small questionnaire study. Poskitt and Wilkinson surveyed 49 people with the condition. The arms were affected in 92%, the legs in 59% and the face in 41%. Another family member had it in 39% of cases.

It also moves with the seasons. In that study, 80% noticed a seasonal swing. Where it changed, it was typically easier in summer and rougher in winter.

What causes keratosis pilaris?

The plug itself is well established: keratin builds up in the opening of the follicle and forms a small, firm cap.

There is often something under it, too. Thomas and Khopkar examined 25 people with KP using a dermatoscope, a handheld magnifier with its own light. In all 25, they found a coiled hair shaft curled inside the plugged follicle. In plain terms, there is often a tiny hair looped beneath the plug. That is why the bump feels like a grain under your fingertip, not a soft spot.

It is also a dry-skin story. Gruber and colleagues compared 20 people with KP to 20 matched controls. The affected patches had a weakened skin barrier and a striking absence of oil glands. That held whether or not the person carried a variant in the filaggrin gene, which helps skin hold water (35% did).

The translation: the bumpy patch is drier and less well sealed than the skin right beside it. That fits the pattern most people notice on their own. Winter air, hot showers and ordinary soap all strip moisture, and the texture gets rougher.

The cause is not fully settled. Researchers still debate whether the plug, the hair or the barrier comes first.

Does keratosis pilaris go away?

Sometimes it eases, and often it simply stays. In the Poskitt and Wilkinson survey, KP had improved with age in 35% of people, stayed the same in 43% and worsened in 22%.

A caution on reading that: the people surveyed were young, aged 18 months to 25 years, and there were only 49 of them. Treat the numbers as a guide, not a guarantee for a 45-year-old.

Why does scrubbing make keratosis pilaris redder?

The instinct is to sand it down. Loofahs, gritty scrubs, dry brushing, picking.

The trouble is that none of these reach the source of the plug. The follicle keeps producing keratin, so the plug simply re-forms. Meanwhile the friction tends to irritate the skin around the follicle. The result is the same bump, now red.

How to smooth keratosis pilaris on arms at home

A short routine, in order of importance:

  1. Stop the friction first. Put the scrub and the brush away, and leave the bumps unpicked.
  2. Keep showers short and lukewarm. Use a gentle non-soap wash on the affected areas.
  3. Moisturise every day, on damp skin. Within a few minutes of towelling off is ideal.
  4. Add a leave-on softening lotion. The family to look for is the keratolytics: urea, lactic acid or salicylic acid. Introduce it slowly, a few nights a week at first. Expect stinging on broken or freshly shaved skin. Wear sunscreen on exposed arms, because lactic acid and other AHAs can raise sun sensitivity.
  5. Give it weeks, not days. Skin tends to look and feel softer over several weeks of steady use, and the bumps return when you stop. This is maintenance. The realistic goal is skin that is softer, smoother-feeling and less noticeable when the sleeves come off.

One caveat worth knowing. A 2024 review of 52 studies, led from UNSW and St George Hospital in Sydney, found that a consistent response has been hard to achieve, and that there is no standard scoring system for measuring improvement. So nobody can honestly promise you a result.

When to see a GP or dermatologist

Book an appointment if the bumps are painful, weeping, spreading quickly or leaving scars, or if you are simply not sure that what you have is KP. A GP or dermatologist can also talk you through prescription and in-clinic options.

A texture, not a flaw

Keratosis pilaris is a texture you look after, not a flaw to be corrected, and it is far more common than the silence around it suggests.
