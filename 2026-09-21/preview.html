Do collagen supplements work? What actually happens between the glass and your skin.

Eating collagen is not a delivery of building material to your face.

Collagen is a protein, and your digestive system does not forward proteins on to wherever you would like them to go. It takes them apart at the door. Enzymes in the stomach and small intestine break dietary protein into short peptides and free amino acids before anything is absorbed. That is not a flaw in the design, it is the design. A collagen molecule you swallow does not arrive in your dermis as a collagen molecule. Nothing you eat does.

So if drinking collagen does anything at all, it has to do it by some other route. (The synthesis side, how skin builds collagen in the first place, is covered separately.)

What actually crosses into the blood when you drink collagen.

Iwai and colleagues measured this in 2005. Volunteers drank gelatin hydrolysates, and the researchers tracked hydroxyproline in their blood. Hydroxyproline is an amino acid found almost exclusively in collagen, so if it turns up, it came from what you drank.

It turned up. The peptide form rose after ingestion, peaked at one to two hours, and had halved by about four hours. The main survivor identified was a two-unit fragment called Pro-Hyp, which is simply proline joined to hydroxyproline.

Two amino acids. That is the traveller, and it lands in the same amino acid pool as the protein from your eggs and your lentils.

Which makes this a signalling question, not a parcel question.

If whole collagen cannot get through, the plausible mechanism is not delivery. It is that those small circulating fragments act as raw material, and possibly as a signal, prompting the cells that build collagen to do more of it.

Possibly. Proposed, not settled. Much of the supporting work sits in cells and animals, and the human chain from a drink to measured new dermal collagen has not been shown end to end.

What the trials found about collagen, wrinkles and skin ageing.

This is where a sceptic expects a debunk. It is not one.

Pu and colleagues, in 2023, pooled 26 randomised controlled trials covering 1,721 people and found that hydrolysed collagen improved skin hydration and elasticity compared with placebo. De Miranda and colleagues, in 2021, pooled 19 trials with 1,125 participants (95 per cent of them women) and reported favourable results for hydration, elasticity and wrinkles at around 90 days. An earlier systematic review by Choi and colleagues in 2019 landed in the same place.

The trials mostly found something.

The fine print that changes how those headlines read.

A gauge moved. That is not the same as a mirror.

Hydration in these trials is corneometry, an electrical reading of moisture at the surface. Elasticity is cutometry, which uses gentle suction to measure how readily skin lifts and returns. Legitimate instruments, both.

A significant change in a cutometer reading is an honest result. It is also not the same claim as your partner noticing. The trials handed back a number off an instrument, and a number moving is the honest version of what happened.

The most-quoted hydration result is weaker inside its own trial.

Proksch and colleagues, in 2014, ran 69 women aged 35 to 55 on 2.5 g or 5 g of collagen peptides daily for eight weeks. Elasticity improved significantly against placebo, which is the finding much of the category rests on. But in the same trial, the moisture and water loss findings did not reach statistical significance. The hydration headline is quoted far more confidently outside that paper than it reads inside it.

Sometimes the trial tested a blend, not collagen.

Bolke and colleagues, in 2019, tested a named commercial drinkable product whose published composition is collagen peptides plus acerola fruit extract, vitamin C, zinc, biotin and a vitamin E complex. It reported improvements in hydration, elasticity, roughness and density. Those results belong to the blend as tested: collagen on its own was never the thing in the bottle.

The authors of the 2023 review also recorded several biases across the trials they pooled, and called for larger ones. That verdict is the reviewers' own, and it should travel with the headline.

One thing that does not hold up.

You will often read that whatever you gain wears off the instant you stop. The trials do not support that as stated. Both studies with a follow-up phase, Proksch 2014 and Bolke 2019, reported some retention four weeks after intake ended. Short follow-ups on small groups, so do not build a philosophy on them. But accuracy beats a tidy ending.

How long does collagen take to work on skin?

Longer than the tub suggests. The trials that report anything at all almost all run 8 to 12 weeks, and the pooled reviews read out at around 90 days. Nothing in that literature describes a visible change in a fortnight, which is roughly when most people decide a supplement is not working and stop.

Three months of daily use, then judge. That is the honest unit of time for this category, and it is the same unit of time that applies to most things you do for skin ageing: consistency is doing more of the work than the label is.

The unglamorous parts matter more than the brand on the tub.

Total protein first. Collagen powder is protein, and for most people the limiting factor is the overall protein and nutrition picture, not the specific source. A scoop bolted onto a thin diet is doing a smaller job than it looks.

Then vitamin C, which is not a wellness garnish here. It is a required cofactor for the enzymes that hydroxylate proline and lysine in new collagen chains, and that step is what lets the triple helix hold its shape (Pullar and colleagues, 2017). No vitamin C, no properly formed helix, whatever else you take.

Collagen painted on the surface is a separate question, and mostly a size problem: the molecule is far too large to cross an intact barrier, so a collagen cream is a good moisturiser and not a structural one.

Four questions to ask a tub.

Take these to the label.

  1. What dose was tested, and does it match the scoop? Trials commonly sit between 2.5 g and 10 g a day. A claim built on 5 g does not travel with a 1 g scoop.
  2. How long did the trial run? The usual readout is 8 to 12 weeks. Judge at 90 days, not at a fortnight.
  3. Was the tested product this product? Or was it a different multi-ingredient formula whose results are being borrowed?
  4. Was the outcome an instrument measurement or a satisfaction survey? Corneometry and cutometry are evidence. "84 per cent of women agreed" is a questionnaire.

Your body does not forward parcels

Your body does not forward parcels. It unpacks everything at the door, and that includes the collagen in your morning coffee.

Swallow it and it does not arrive at your face as collagen. It is a protein, so it gets taken apart on the way in. What researchers have actually recovered from blood afterwards is tiny two-unit fragments, rising for an hour or two and roughly halving by four hours. Small pieces, not collagen.

Think flat-pack. You cannot post a finished wardrobe through a doorway that narrow. What arrives is timber and fixings, and what gets built depends on everything else happening on site.

Now the fair part, and it matters. The 2023 Nutrients meta-analysis pooled 26 randomised trials and more than 1,700 people, and found hydrolysed collagen improved measured skin hydration and elasticity against placebo. Worth saying plainly.

The catch is in the fine print. Those are instrument readings taken over 8 to 12 weeks, not before-and-after in the mirror.

And the trial quoted is not always a trial of the tub. In one widely quoted study the hydration result did not reach statistical significance. In another, the product tested was a blend with vitamin C, zinc, biotin and vitamin E alongside the collagen, so the win belongs to the formula, not the powder.

Four questions do most of the work here. What dose was tested. How long did the trial run. Was the tested product this product. Was the result an instrument reading or a satisfaction survey.
