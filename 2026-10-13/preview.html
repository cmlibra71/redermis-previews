Does Drinking Water Hydrate Your Skin? What the Research Shows

Two extra litres of water a day for a month, and the women who drank the least to begin with showed the biggest change in their skin hydration readings. It is a tidy result, and it gets quoted a lot. But the measurement that did not move tells you more than the one that did.

Does drinking water hydrate your skin?

A little, mostly if you drink little to begin with. The effect in the research is slight, measured mostly in younger adults, and it is not a substitute for keeping water in your skin once it is there. Think of drinking as topping up the supply, not fixing the leak.

How does water reach your skin?

Skin is built in layers, and water reaches them differently.

The deeper living layers sit on top of the dermis, which is threaded with tiny blood vessels. Those vessels carry fluid and nutrients up from the body, so the living cells are supplied from below, a bit like groundwater rising through soil.

The outer layer, the stratum corneum, is another story. It is made of flattened, mostly dead cells stacked like bricks, with lipids (skin fats) acting as the mortar. These cells hold water only as well as that mortar and the skin's natural moisturising factors allow. Water leaves this layer by evaporating from the top, the way moisture lifts off a paddock on a warm day.

So the picture is simple: water arrives from below and leaves from the top. Any question about hydrating skin is really two questions. How much is arriving, and how fast is it leaving?

It also helps to separate two words that get used interchangeably. Dehydrated skin is short on water and can happen to any skin type, oily included, while dry skin is a skin type that makes fewer of its own oils.

What happened when people drank more?

Two studies from the same research group are the ones most often cited.

In the first (Palma and colleagues, Clinical, Cosmetic and Investigational Dermatology, 2015), 49 healthy young women (average age about 24) drank about 2 litres extra a day for 30 days. Superficial and deep skin hydration readings rose, most in those who usually drank less.

In the second (Palma and colleagues, Skin Research and Technology, 2015), 34 young women followed the same plan of 2 litres a day for 30 days. Hydration rose in the lower drinkers. There was no significant change in transepidermal water loss.

Two details are worth holding onto. These are instrument readings of hydration, not photographs of plumper or smoother skin. And the biggest shift appeared in people who started out drinking less, which makes sense: if you are already well supplied, there is little room to improve.

What does the wider evidence say?

A single study is a data point. A systematic review asks what happens when you gather everything available.

Akdeniz and colleagues (Skin Research and Technology, 2018) reviewed six studies on dietary fluid intake and skin hydration in healthy adults. Their conclusion was measured. The evidence was weak in quantity and methodological quality. Increased intake was linked to a slight increase in hydration, particularly in people with lower prior water intake. The underlying mechanism is unknown.

One more point lands close to home if you are in your 30s, 40s, 50s or beyond. Across the studies in the review, average ages ranged from about 24 to 56, mostly younger adults, and whether the effect holds in older adults is unclear. It is simply a gap: the research has mostly been done on younger skin. Skin changes with time (the outer layer's lipids and natural moisturising factors shift), and that may well matter for how any of this plays out.

Does drinking water reduce transepidermal water loss (TEWL)?

TEWL stands for transepidermal water loss. It is the rate at which water evaporates out through your skin into the air.

In that second study, drinking more raised hydration readings but did not significantly change TEWL.

Picture a pot of water on a low heat with the lid off. Pouring in more water keeps the level up for a while, but the steam keeps escaping. Putting the lid on is what changes the rate of loss. For skin, the "lid" is a healthy barrier, and this is where everyday routine earns its place: a gentle cleanser that does not strip the surface, and a moisturiser that helps hold water in. Those work on the leaving side, which is the side drinking cannot reach.

Quick reference: supply side vs leak side

  • Supply side: water arrives from below, through the blood vessels in the dermis.
  • What helps supply: drinking enough, especially if you usually drink little.
  • Leak side: water evaporates out through the stratum corneum at the surface.
  • What helps the leak: a gentle cleanser, and moisturiser applied while skin is still slightly damp.

How much water should you drink for healthy skin?

None of this means water does not matter. You need fluids for your body to function, and skin is part of the body. It just means water is one half of the picture. A practical, low-fuss approach:

  • Drink to thirst. Thirst and pale-straw coloured urine are everyday cues that most healthy people can use without counting anything.
  • Spread it through the day. Sipping steadily is easier to sustain than catching up in one go.
  • Notice if you drink very little. That is the group the studies suggest may notice a small difference in hydration readings.
  • Seal the surface. After washing, apply moisturiser while skin is still slightly damp, so it can help hold in the water that is already there.

You may have heard that eight glasses a day is the target. Treat that as a rule of thumb, not a skin finding. The research above did not test it, and individual needs vary with body size, climate, activity and diet.

One health note: anyone who has been advised to restrict fluids, for example because of a heart or kidney condition, should follow their GP's advice rather than any general guidance.

Quick questions

Does coffee or tea count?

Both contribute to your overall fluid intake, so they are part of the picture. We have not cited research on how they compare with plain water for skin, so we will not claim one.

Can you over-drink for your skin?

There is no sign in this research of any skin benefit beyond normal, comfortable drinking. More is not better here, so there is no reason to push past thirst for the sake of your complexion.

How long before you would notice?

The studies took their readings at 15 and 30 days. Those were measurements by instruments, not visible changes you would necessarily see in the mirror, so it is fair to expect subtle at most.

The takeaway

Drinking water supports the supply side of skin hydration, and it may make a small difference if you tend to drink little. It does not slow the water leaving through the surface, which is why caring for your barrier matters just as much. Keep both sides in mind, and the question of how much water becomes much less fraught.
