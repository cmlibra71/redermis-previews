Does Shaving or Dermaplaning Make Facial Hair Grow Back Thicker? What the Blade Actually Removes

Meta description: Does facial hair grow back thicker after shaving or dermaplaning? Usually not. Here is where hair is made, and how to do it gently.

The razor sits in the drawer. One worry keeps it there: will it grow back thicker? It will not. The blade never reaches the part of the hair that decides.

Hair is not made at the surface. It is made well below it, in a structure the razor never touches. Here is why regrowth can look coarser anyway, what the blade really takes off, and how to do it without upsetting your skin.

Why shaved hair seems to grow back thicker (but does not).

The myth survives because it looks true. There are three reasons.

First, the shape of the tip. A hair that has never been cut tapers to a fine, soft point. Cut it and you leave a blunt end, the full width of the shaft. As that blunt end pushes back through the skin, it feels coarser under your fingertips, even though the hair itself is the same diameter it always was.

Second, colour. Hair that has been sitting on your face for weeks has been lightened a little by sun and daily wear. Fresh regrowth has not, so it can look darker for a while.

Third, posture. Short stubble stands straight up before it grows long enough to lie flat, which makes it more noticeable.

What actually sets a hair's thickness, colour and growth rate is the follicle: a small pocket of living tissue sitting in the dermis, the layer beneath the surface. The blade only touches the shaft, the part above the skin, which is made of keratin and is no longer alive. Think of mowing a lawn. The grass looks blunt and even afterwards, but the roots carry on exactly as before.

This is not just logic. In a small controlled study published in 1970, Lynfield and MacWilliams compared shaved and unshaved areas and found shaving did not change the rate of regrowth or the width of the hair. It is an old, modest study, but the biology behind it has held up for decades.

Peach fuzz vs terminal hair: the two kinds on your face.

Your face carries two broad types of hair.

Vellus hair is the fine, short, lightly pigmented "peach fuzz" covering most of the face. You mostly notice it in low sunlight, or when foundation catches on it.

Terminal hair is coarser, longer and darker. Your brows are terminal hair, and for many women so are a few strands on the chin or upper lip.

Hormones, not razors, can gradually shift some follicles towards producing terminal hair, which is often noticed around perimenopause. That is simply worth knowing. If coarse facial hair appears suddenly, is new, or spreads quickly, it is worth a conversation with your GP.

What dermaplaning removes besides hair.

Dermaplaning means drawing a fine blade across the skin at a low angle. It takes off two things: vellus hair, and loose cells from the stratum corneum, the outermost layer of the skin, which is made of flattened cells already on their way out.

That is why skin can look smoother and more even straight afterwards, and why makeup often sits flatter. There is less fuzz for powder to cling to and fewer loose flakes for foundation to settle into.

It also means dermaplaning is a physical exfoliation. It counts towards your exfoliation load for the week, in the same budget as acids, scrubs and retinoids. Your skin sheds and replaces these surface cells continuously, so the smooth effect is temporary by design.

How to dermaplane or shave your face without irritating it.

Done well, it is a gentle step. Done carelessly, it can leave skin tight, pink and stinging. A few habits make the difference.

  • Start clean. Cleanse and dry the skin fully, or use a light, slippery prep if your blade is designed for it.
  • Use a fresh or cleaned blade. A dull blade drags, and a dirty one can carry bacteria.
  • Hold it low. Keep the blade at a shallow angle, roughly 20 to 30 degrees, never upright like a knife.
  • Short strokes, light pressure. Work in small sections, in the direction of hair growth. Let the blade do the work.
  • Skip the tricky spots. Avoid active breakouts, broken or sunburnt skin, and anywhere that is already irritated.
  • Pause other exfoliants. Leave acids and retinoids out for a day or two either side so you are not stacking exfoliation.
  • Keep the rest of the night simple. A plain moisturiser is plenty. Leave other active steps for another night.

How often should you dermaplane your face?

A common rhythm for full-face dermaplaning is every three to four weeks, which roughly tracks how quickly surface cells and vellus hair return. That is common practice rather than a clinical rule, so let your skin set the pace. Quickly tidying a few chin hairs between sessions is a different job and far less exfoliating.

Whatever the rhythm, sunscreen the next morning and every morning. Freshly exfoliated skin has lost some of its outer buffer, and with Australian spring UV climbing, this is the week it matters most.

Who might skip it, or check first.

Dermaplaning is not for every skin, and that is fine.

  • Breakout-prone skin. A blade can nick inflamed spots and spread bacteria across the face. Wait until the skin is calm.
  • Very reactive skin. If your skin flushes or stings easily, physical exfoliation may be more than it wants.
  • Prescription retinoids or recent procedures. Skin on prescription retinoids, or recovering from peels, laser or injectables, is already working hard. Check with whoever prescribed or performed it first.

The short version.

It will not grow back thicker.

It is exfoliation, so budget it.

The follicle, not the blade, decides.
