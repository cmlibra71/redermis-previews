Skin after flying: what a long-haul flight does to your skin (and why drinking more water only goes so far)

The driest room you will ever sit in for fourteen hours.

Somewhere over the Indian Ocean, you reach for the lip balm for the fourth time and notice your cheeks feel papery under your fingertips. By the time you land, your skin looks dull, a little flaky around the nose, and oddly puffy under the eyes.

It is not your imagination. It is not your skincare failing, either. It is physics, and a fair bit of it is predictable.

Why cabin air dries out your skin.

At cruising altitude, the air outside the plane is extremely cold, and cold air holds very little water. That outside air is what gets drawn in, pressurised, warmed and circulated through the cabin, and it is refreshed constantly. Warm it up and you get air with a very low relative humidity.

How low? In one small study that measured it on long-distance flights, cabin relative humidity dropped below 10% within two hours of take-off and stayed there for the rest of the flight. For comparison, a comfortable home usually sits well above that.

Now the skin part. The outermost layer of your skin, the stratum corneum, is a thin deck of flattened, dead cells held together by fats. It is not sealed off from the room. It holds water partly by balancing with the humidity around it, a little like a sponge left out on the kitchen bench. In humid air it stays supple. In very dry air, water leaves the surface faster than the layers below can top it up.

Tight, rough, dull, a bit itchy: that is what a thirsty surface layer feels like.

What the measurements show.

The study mentioned above, by Guéhenneux and colleagues (2012), followed eight women aged 29 to 39 on long-distance flights. They skipped skincare from 12 hours beforehand and throughout the flight, so the researchers could see what the cabin did on its own. Using a standard surface-hydration measure, they found hydration fell rapidly on both the face and forearms, with the biggest change on the cheeks, where it dropped by up to 37%.

A broader review by Engebretsen and colleagues (2016) pulls together the wider picture: low humidity and low temperatures lead to a general decrease in skin barrier function, and skin becomes more reactive to irritants. Dry, cold conditions are also linked with flares in people prone to eczema.

Worth keeping in perspective. The flight study was small, and it measured surface hydration, not lasting damage. What it tells us is direction and speed: the cabin pulls water from the surface, and it does it quickly.

Why your face looks puffy when you land.

The puffiness is a different story from the dryness. Hours of sitting still, lower cabin pressure, salty plane food and a glass or two of wine all nudge fluid around the body, and gravity plus stillness decide where it collects. Ankles are the classic spot. Faces often join in, especially under the eyes.

For most people it is cosmetic and temporary. Moving when you can, and following your airline's in-flight movement advice, helps it settle faster.

Does drinking water on a plane help your skin?

"Just drink more water on the plane" is only partly true.

Drinking water matters for you as a whole: long flights are dehydrating, and staying on top of fluids is sensible. But the outermost layer of skin is made of dead cells. It is not quickly re-wetted from the inside. It is losing water from the outside, to the air, and a bottle of water does little to slow that down in the short term.

What slows it is a layer on top. A rich moisturiser or an occlusive (think balms, or creams with ingredients like petrolatum, shea butter or squalane) sits over the surface and helps hold water in. On a plane, that layer is doing most of the heavy lifting.

So: drink the water. Seal the skin as well.

A travel-day routine for long-haul flight skin.

Save this for your next trip. It is simpler than it looks.

  1. Moisturise before you board. Apply a generous layer of your usual moisturiser, ideally a slightly richer one than normal, before you leave for the airport.
  2. Reapply small amounts mid-flight. A pea-sized top-up every few hours on a long-haul leg helps more than one thick layer at the start. Decant into a travel-sized pot.
  3. Do not forget lips and hands. Lips have almost no oil of their own, and the backs of your hands get washed repeatedly in plane bathrooms. A balm covers both.
  4. Skip new or strong actives around the flight. In the 24 to 48 hours either side of a long flight, it is sensible not to start a new retinoid or exfoliating acid. Stick to what your skin already knows.
  5. Mist, then seal. A face mist on its own evaporates in dry air and can leave skin feeling tighter than before. If you love a mist, follow it with moisturiser straight away.
  6. Window seat in daylight? SPF. UVA passes through aircraft windows, so a quick layer of sunscreen on exposed skin is an easy habit for daytime flights.
  7. On arrival, keep it gentle. A mild cleanse, moisturiser and, if you can manage it, sleep. Your skin will thank you for boring.

Skin usually resets.

The good news is that most post-flight skin is short-lived. Once you are back in normal air with your usual routine, the surface layer rebalances its water, and the tightness and dullness typically settle within a day or two. Puffiness usually eases as you move around.

If something persists, hurts, spreads or looks like a rash rather than dryness, that is a conversation for your GP or pharmacist, not a travel-routine tweak.

What a long flight actually does to your skin

By hour nine of a long flight, your face can feel tight and dull. That is usually the cabin air, not your skincare.

Cabin air on a long-haul flight is drier than many deserts: humidity is often around 10 to 20 per cent, and in one small study it fell below 10% within two hours. The outer layer of your skin (the stratum corneum) constantly trades water with the air around it. Put it in a very dry room for 14 hours and it keeps giving water away.

A 2012 study in Skin Research and Technology measured this on long-distance flights. Hydration dropped quickly.

Now the myth. Drinking water is good for you, so keep doing it. But it does not quickly re-wet that outer layer. Sealing it does more.

A few travel-day habits:
1. Moisturise before you board, then top up halfway through.
2. Do lips and hands too. They dry out first.
3. Skip new or strong actives the day or two either side of a long flight.
4. Face mist only helps if you seal it with moisturiser straight after.

The good news: for most people it settles within a day or two of landing.
