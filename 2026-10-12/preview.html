Does \"non-comedogenic\" mean anything? Where pore-clogging ratings come from

What does non-comedogenic mean? It means a product is formulated with the aim of not blocking pores (a blocked pore is a comedone). It is a claim on the pack, not a standardised test result, so it is a reasonable hint rather than a guarantee.

You try a new foundation or moisturiser. Three days later there is a spot on your chin. The product goes in the bin, and you cross it off the list for good.

Here is the plain claim: that product is often not the culprit. A blocked pore builds like a traffic jam that started kilometres back. By the time you see the brake lights, the cause is long gone from the scene.

How long does a clogged pore take to form?

Every pore is the opening of a hair follicle, a tiny channel that carries oil (sebum) up to the surface. Dead skin cells line that channel. Most days they shed normally and leave with the oil.

Sometimes the cells stick together instead, and mix with sebum into a plug. That plug builds slowly, below the surface. The early version is called a microcomedone: a plug too small and too deep to see yet. Once it grows, it becomes a comedone, which is just the technical word for a blocked pore. Open at the surface, it is a blackhead. Closed under the skin, it is a whitehead.

How long does the whole process take? Usually weeks, not days. That is general knowledge about how skin cycles, so treat it as a rough guide rather than a stopwatch. The point is that a spot appearing three days after a new product may well have been forming before you opened the jar.

Where do comedogenic ratings come from?

You have probably seen the lists: ingredients graded from 0 to 5, shared as screenshots, with coconut oil near the top and something else near the bottom. They have a real history, and it is a respectable one.

In 1972, Albert Kligman and Otto Mills described "acne cosmetica", the idea that some cosmetics could provoke blocked pores in some people. It was an important observation. It also raised a practical problem: how do you screen thousands of ingredients without recruiting thousands of volunteers?

The answer was the rabbit ear. The assay put ingredients and products on rabbit ears and looked for the blocked follicles that followed. In 1984, Fulton and colleagues published long lists of ingredients ranked by how the rabbit ear responded. Notably, the authors themselves described the rabbit ear assay as "not an ideal animal model but is the best we have". They knew the limits. It was the best tool available at the time, and it produced real data.

The trouble is what happened next. The numbers were separated from the caveats, rounded into a tidy 0 to 5 scale, and passed around the internet as if they described how an ingredient behaves on your face.

Are comedogenic ratings (0 to 5) reliable?

An ingredient score describes a raw ingredient in an animal model. A product you buy is something else. It is an ingredient at a few percent, sitting inside an emulsion with water, emollients, stabilisers and preservatives. Concentration and formulation change how a substance behaves on skin. Reading a finished product's ingredient list and adding up the scores is a bit like judging a cake by tasting the flour.

This is more than a theory. In 2006, Zoe Draelos and Joseph DiNardo re-evaluated the comedogenicity concept in humans. Six people had finished products applied to the upper back, three times a week for four weeks, and the researchers counted microcomedones. Their conclusion: finished products using comedogenic ingredients are not necessarily comedogenic.

The limits matter here. It was six people and a small set of products, so it is not the last word on any single moisturiser. What it does show is that an ingredient's rating does not reliably predict what a finished product does.

The label does not fill the gap either. "Non-comedogenic" on a pack does not tell you how, or whether, the finished product was tested. It is a reasonable hint. It is not a result.

How to test if a product clogs your pores

If the lists and the label are weak evidence, what is strong evidence? Your own skin, observed patiently. Here is a simple way to run that.

Change one thing at a time. If you start a new cleanser, serum and moisturiser in the same week, you will never know which one to credit or blame. Add one product, leave the rest alone.

Give it about four weeks. This is a sensible window rather than a rule. It roughly matches the four-week human test above, and it respects how slowly pores clog. A spot in week one is weak evidence against a product, because it may already have been forming.

Note where spots appear. Cheekbones, jaw and hairline can tell different stories. Spots along the hairline, for instance, sometimes point to hair products or a pillowcase rather than your face cream. A quick note in your phone each week is enough.

Look for a pattern, not a single spot. One blemish proves very little. A steady increase in the same area across several weeks is more informative.

None of this is a guarantee. Skin varies, hormones and stress play a part, and sometimes a product really is the cause. The aim is only to make that call on better evidence than a screenshot.

When should you see a doctor about breakouts?

If breakouts are persistent, painful or leaving marks, it is worth seeing a GP or dermatologist rather than experimenting on your own.
