Sunscreen gaps: the eyelids, hairline and ears your routine keeps missing

Under a UV camera, the skin people miss most with sunscreen is the eyelids. Most routines are built for the middle of the face, and the hairline, the eyelids and the ears get whatever is left over.

Your skincare routine has a border, and it is usually drawn short of where your face actually ends.

That is not carelessness. It is habit. We apply products in front of a mirror, so our hands go where our eyes go: the centre. We steer around the eyes because product stings. Hair covers the top and sides, so we work around it. And the edges are simply where we stop rubbing, the point where the last of the product runs out.

Forehead, cheeks, nose, chin. Done.

The skin you skip is still skin. It sees the same sun, collects the same residue, and over time it shows it. The good news is that closing the gaps takes a few small changes, not a new routine.

Where sunscreen gets missed most: the eyelids.

This is one of the few edge-of-face habits that has actually been measured. Researchers at the University of Liverpool photographed 57 people with a UV-sensitive camera after they applied sunscreen the way they normally would. Sunscreen absorbs UV, so covered skin shows up dark in the image and missed skin shows up pale. The median person missed about 14% of the eyelid region, compared with about 7% of the rest of the face. When participants were given public-health style instructions and tried again, eyelid coverage improved, but the inner corner of the eye (the medial canthus, beside the bridge of the nose) was still often left bare (Pratt et al., 2017).

A follow-up study with 84 people compared sunscreen with SPF moisturiser. The moisturiser covered less of the face, mostly at the eyelids, where roughly 21% was missed compared with about 14% for sunscreen. Just as telling, participants generally did not realise their coverage was incomplete (Lourenco et al., 2019).

Put plainly: the thinnest skin on the face tends to get the thinnest protection. If you have already worked out how much sunscreen to use, this is the other half of the story. Not how much, but where it lands.

A few everyday fixes help:

  • Give the eye area its own step. Use a small amount of an eye-safe, fragrance-free formula or a sunscreen stick, and pat it along the lid and into the inner corner with your ring finger.
  • Do not lean on an SPF moisturiser alone for days outdoors. It is better than nothing, but it is easy to under-apply around the eyes.
  • Wear sunglasses that wrap. The study authors themselves suggest UV-blocking sunglasses as a sensible partner to sunscreen, because they cover exactly the zone our hands tend to avoid.

Why do I get breakouts along my hairline?

The hairline has two separate problems: what lands on it, and what does not.

Residue

Conditioner, hair oils, styling creams and dry shampoo do not stay politely on the hair. They migrate onto the skin where hair meets the forehead and temples, and that strip is often the last place a cleanser reaches. Dermatologists have described breakouts along the hairline linked to heavy hair products since 1970, when Plewig, Fulton and Kligman named the pattern "pomade acne". If your skin is breakout-prone along the forehead or temples, your hair routine is worth a look before your face routine.

  • Wash your hair before you cleanse your face in the shower, so conditioner is not left sitting on freshly cleansed skin.
  • Rinse conditioner off your face deliberately, tipping your head back and away from the stream.
  • Pull your hair back with a headband before you cleanse, so the cleanser reaches the true hairline, not just below it.
  • Keep styling products about a finger-width back from the hairline.

Sun

The hairline, the temples and any part line are easy to skip with sunscreen, partly because nobody wants it in their hair. Outdoors, a hat does most of the work. On longer days in the sun, a light spray or powder SPF along the part covers what a hat might not.

Do your ears and jaw need sunscreen too?

The ears, the patch of skin just in front of them and the area under the angle of the jaw rarely get a thorough cleanse or a full layer of sunscreen. They still catch plenty of sun outdoors, the tops of the ears especially.

Two small habits close this gap. Give the ears (and the neck below them) a fresh dab of sunscreen of their own, rather than whatever is left after the cheeks. And finish every cleanse with one slow pass along the jaw edge and behind the ears before you rinse.

A routine that reaches the edges: a checklist worth saving.

  1. Headband first. Hair back before any cleanser or sunscreen goes on.
  2. Hair before face. In the shower, wash and condition first, then cleanse your face last.
  3. Give the edges their own dose. Do the face, then take a fresh pea of sunscreen for the hairline and temples, the eyelids and inner corners, and the ears and jaw edge, so the edges get product of their own, not what is left.
  4. A dedicated eye-area product. A stick or eye-safe formula makes the lids and inner corners easier to reach without stinging.
  5. Hat and wrap sunglasses outdoors. They protect the exact zones fingers tend to miss.

None of this is dramatic. That is the point. Skin longevity is mostly built from small, repeated habits, and a routine that is consistent at the edges does more over the years than one that is intensive in the middle and absent everywhere else.
